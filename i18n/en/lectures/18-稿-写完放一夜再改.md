# Lecture 18 · The Draft: Let It Sit Overnight, Then Revise

After seventeen lectures, you have seventeen moves. The last lecture teaches no new move. It teaches how to use them on your own drafts.

A draft you've just finished looks good all over to you, because you remember what you meant to say, and your mind fills in whatever didn't make it onto the page. The reader doesn't have your mind.

## 1. The one move to learn in this lecture

> **When you finish, let it sit overnight. The next day, treat yourself as a stranger, read it aloud from the top, and revise against a checklist.**

The test: **wherever you stumble or want to skip ahead on the second-day read, that is where to revise.**

## 2. How the masters revise

Many writers have a habit of letting things cool: finish, lock it in a drawer, take it out again after a while and revise. Accounts of how particular writers revised vary, so this lecture takes only the practice and quotes no one.

**While writing you are the author; while revising you have to be the reader.** The night in between is for changing roles.

## 3. What AI writes

A machine doesn't look back when it finishes, and never feels that something is off. Its first draft is its last, so you can always find in it every fault the previous seventeen lectures have covered.

That makes AI drafts good practice material for revision: **plenty of faults, so your hands get quick.**

## 4. One revision: a checklist

On the second-day read, go through these in order. Each item maps to a lecture:

| Check | Lecture |
|---|---|
| Is the first sentence a moral? | Lecture 1 · Opening |
| Is it full of adjectives and short on actions? | Lecture 2 · Lines, Lecture 8 · People |
| Does the ending elevate? Can the most heartfelt line be cut? | Lecture 3 · Closing, Lecture 15 · The Unsaid |
| Cut 30% of the words: is the meaning still there? | Lecture 4 · Revision |
| Are the characters speaking your words? | Lecture 5 · Speech |
| Is what the reader most wants to know in the first paragraph? | Lecture 9 · Order |
| Is there an event behind each argument? | Lecture 10 · Argument |
| Does the voice change partway through? | Lecture 11 · Voice |
| Do you climb into two people's heads? | Lecture 13 · Viewpoint |
| Does it turn more than once? | Lecture 16 · The Turn |
| Has the most important summary been broken into a scene? | Lecture 17 · The Scene |
| Could the title be moved onto someone else's piece? | Lecture 12 · Titles |

## 5. Your exercise

Of all the exercises in these eighteen lectures, pick the one you're happiest with and let it sit overnight. The next day:

1. Read it aloud once and underline wherever you stumble.
2. Go through the checklist item by item, making at most one change per item.
3. Put the before and after side by side and write three sentences: what you changed, why you changed it, and which item you fall foul of most often.

That last item is the fault to keep watching for over the coming year. **The other half of style is knowing where your faults are.**
