# Lecture 3 · Closing: Don't Rise to a Moral at the End

The part of a piece that most often gives it away as AI is the last paragraph.

> This is the meaning of life. May we all find our own light in ordinary days.

The problem isn't that it's badly written. **It sums up on the reader's behalf.** The reader finished the piece with a feeling of their own, and this sentence covers it over.

A good ending does one thing: **it leaves the reader at the scene and lets them walk out on their own.**

## 1. The one move this lecture teaches

> **Delete every summarizing word in the last paragraph. Stop on an action, an object, or something someone actually said.**

The test: **if the ending could be clipped out and posted as a social-media caption, delete it.**

"May we all…" is a caption. "He pulled the door shut behind him and left" is an ending.

## 2. How the masters do it

Lu Xun ends Kong Yiji's story with one sentence:

> 大约孔乙己的确死了。
> *Translation:* Kong Yiji is probably dead, indeed.

Nine characters. No mourning, no summary, no explanation of why "probably" and "indeed" sit side by side. **The uncertainty in that sentence weighs more than any lament.**

Su Shi doesn't rise to a moral at the end of "A Night Visit to Chengtian Temple" either. He says only 但少闲人如吾两人者耳 (*Translation:* it's only that there are few idle men like the two of us). Not "friendship is precious," but "idle men like us are rare." He presses the feeling into one judgment, and stops.

Wang Zengqi often ends on a plain remark or an image: the talking is over, and the person is still standing there. He doesn't tell you what to be moved by.

What they share: **something remains after the ending.** Readers keep thinking on their own; a moral blocks that road.

## 3. What AI writes

Machine endings fall into three set patterns, by frequency:

1. **Stating the theme**: "This is the meaning of…", "…is what true … really is."
2. **The wish**: "May we all…", "I hope everyone who … can…"
3. **The call**: "Let us…", "Why not start today…"

All three do the same thing: **they close the piece down to zero.** The author is afraid the reader missed the point, so he pastes the answer at the end.

The real issue: **the reader doesn't need you to worry that he missed it.** If you're worried, you didn't write enough earlier.

## 4. One revision

**Original**
> The years flowed by, but the taste of that bowl of noodles stayed in my heart. It made me understand that even in the most ordinary days lies the most precious warmth. May we all cherish the people around us and cherish every moment.

**Breakdown**: three summary words (understand, precious, cherish), one wish, zero images. The whole piece's feeling is spent in one go.

**Revised**
> When I went back last year, the street had been torn down. Where the noodle shop used to be is a bubble-tea place now, with two tall stools by the door. I stood there a while and didn't go in.

Three actions in the revision: torn down, stools set out, standing a while without going in. **Not one word says nostalgia.** But the reader knows.

**The key difference**: the original tells readers "now be moved." The revision leaves them a path to walk in on their own.

## 5. Your exercise

Cut the ending below to 35 words or fewer. (The Chinese original sets 50 characters.) **You may not use**: understand, meaning, precious, warmth, may (as in a wish), cherish, hope, worth.

> This experience taught me a great deal. Life isn't easy, but as long as you hold on to hope, dawn will always come. May everyone who works hard at living be treated gently.

**Three constraints:**

1. End on a single action or a single image.
2. No summarizing sentence of any kind.
3. The last sentence must be short: 10 words or fewer. (The Chinese original sets 15 characters.)

**Self-check**: copy your ending out and send it to yourself. **If it looks like a line you could pair with a photo and post, rewrite it.**
