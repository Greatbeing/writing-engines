# Lecture 9 · Order: Say First What the Reader Most Wants to Know

When all the material in a piece is right and it is still tiring to read, the order is usually wrong. Beginners write in **the order things happened**: that morning I got up, had breakfast, took the bus... and the reader learns what happened only in paragraph five.

## 1. The one move this lecture teaches

> **Before you write, ask: what does the reader most want to know? Put it in the first paragraph. Order the rest by "what will they ask next?"**

The test: **at the end of every paragraph a question forms in the reader's mind, and the next paragraph answers it.**

## 2. How the masters do it

Lu Xun's "Mr. Fujino" (《藤野先生》) is about his studies in Sendai, but it does not begin with going abroad:

> 东京也无非是这样。

*Translation:* Tokyo was no more than this, after all.

One sentence hands over the thing the reader most wants: his attitude. He is disappointed in Tokyo. The reader naturally asks: then where did he go? The next paragraph is Sendai.

Journalism calls this the "inverted pyramid": the most important thing goes first. An essay needn't be that rigid, but the principle holds: **order is arranged for the reader, not for your memory.**

## 3. What AI writes

> With the development of society, the pace of people's lives is getting faster and faster. Against this background, more and more young people are paying attention to mental health. This article will explore the following aspects...

Three sentences of setup, none of them something the reader wants to know. Readers of a piece that opens with "With the development of..." rarely reach the fourth sentence.

## 4. One revision

**Original** (in chronological order)
> Last Saturday my friend and I went hiking. We left at seven, sat in traffic for an hour, and reached the foot of the mountain at nine. Halfway up, my friend said his knee hurt; only later did I learn he had injured his meniscus long before.

**Breakdown**: the real story is the last sentence; the first three are a log.

**Revised**
> My friend waited until we were halfway up the mountain to tell me he had torn his meniscus two years earlier. We had left at seven and sat in traffic for an hour, and he never mentioned it the whole way.

Give the reader what they want first, then go back and fill in the time. The filled-in lines now mean something: he never mentioned it the whole way.

## 5. Your exercise

Take something you wrote recently (an essay, a weekly report, a social media post). **Three steps:**

1. In one sentence, write down "what the reader most wants to know."
2. Find which paragraph of the original it sits in.
3. Move it to the first paragraph, reorder the rest by "what will the reader ask next," and cut any paragraph that answers no question.

When you're done, count: you can usually cut about a third.
