# Lecture 4 · Revision: Cut the Words You Can Do Without

The first three lectures were about how to write. This one is about what to do after you've written.

Asked about method, Lu Xun once gave a very practical answer:

> 写完后至少看两遍，竭力将可有可无的字，句，段删去，毫不可惜。
> *Translation:* When you've finished, read it over at least twice, and do your utmost to cut any word, sentence, or paragraph that can be done without. Don't regret it.

Look at the sentence itself: no "I believe," no "this is very important." He had already cut it himself.

## 1. The one move this lecture teaches

> **Once it's written, set it aside. When you come back, only subtract: ask of every sentence, "If I cut this, what would the reader no longer know?" If you can't answer, cut it.**

The test: **the revised draft is 30% shorter than the first, and nothing in the meaning is lost.** If you can't get it shorter, you're still holding on.

## 2. How the masters do it

Su Shi's "A Night Visit to Chengtian Temple" runs to fewer than a hundred characters in all: one night, one friend, one stretch of moonlight, one remark.

He doesn't say why he was exiled to Huangzhou, who Zhang Huaimin was, or what they talked about that night. All of that is "can be done without": important to him, not to this piece.

**Cutting isn't cutting bad sentences. It's cutting good sentences that don't belong to this piece.** That's the hardest part.

## 3. What AI writes

Machine drafts carry three kinds of padding, all easy to spot:

1. **Wind-up**: "Speaking of…, one has to mention…", "As everyone knows…" The text hasn't started yet.
2. **Restatement**: the same point said three ways; the second and third are there to fill space.
3. **Hedging**: "to a certain extent," "in a sense," "one might say." The author won't commit, so every sentence gets a cushion.

None of these are wrong sentences, which is why beginners hate to cut them. But each empty sentence costs the next one a little of the reader's trust.

## 4. One revision

**Original**
> Speaking of childhood, one has to mention the old locust tree outside my grandmother's door. In a sense, that tree kept me company through my whole childhood, witnessed my growing up, and was an indispensable part of my childhood memories.

**Breakdown**: one wind-up (speaking of… one has to mention), one hedge (in a sense), three restatements (kept me company, witnessed, indispensable: all the same point). The real information is one fact: there's an old locust tree outside my grandmother's door.

**Revised**
> There's an old locust tree outside my grandmother's door. The year I was seven I climbed it, couldn't get down, and sat up there until dark.

More than half the words are gone, and one event has been added. **Revising isn't only cutting. It's giving the space you cleared to something specific.**

## 5. Your exercise

Take something you've written yourself (an essay, a weekly report, a long social-media post) and pick a passage of about 200 words. (The Chinese original sets about 300 characters.)

**Three constraints:**

1. The revision may be no more than 70% of the original's word count.
2. Delete every "in a sense," "to a certain extent," "I have to say," and "as everyone knows."
3. Add at least one specific detail the original didn't have (a time, a number, something someone said).

**Self-check**: put the two versions side by side. **If you don't miss a single line of the 30% you cut, you cut right.** If you truly miss a line, put it back. That one is yours.
