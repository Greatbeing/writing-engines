# The First Workers to Use “Li Na” as a Full Stop Have Already Seen Through It All

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Office workers these days have, to a man, been diagnosed with a new verbal habit. Nothing serious. It’s just that halfway through a sentence in a chat, they suddenly tack on two characters.

“Don’t want to go to work again today… Li Na.”

“How did Saturday go by so fast… Li Na.”

“It’s four-thirty… Li Na.”

At first glance it looks like they’re calling someone; look closer and it doesn’t. Call it a complaint, and it curses no one; call it confiding, and it tells nothing. A sentence that was flat to begin with gets these two characters added and suddenly takes on a wistful taste, like a sigh packed into a full stop.

This generation of young people has started using a name as a punctuation mark.

## “I’ve drawn a circle on the ground and made it my prison, Li Na”

The Li Na construction [厉娜体, “Li Na style”] has been viral online for more than half a month. Everyone knows the source by now: the Mango TV reality show *Hou Hui You Qi* [《后会友期》, a pun on 后会有期, “we’ll meet again”, with 有 “have” swapped for its homophone 友 “friend”], in which Xu Fei and Li Na, contestants from the 2006 season of *Super Girl*, talked on a clifftop after nearly twenty years out of contact. Xu Fei ended every line with “Li Na”:

“I’ve drawn a circle on the ground and made it my prison, Li Na.” [画地为牢, a four-character idiom: “to draw a circle on the ground as a prison”.]

“I’ve waited fifteen years for this moment, Li Na.”

The line that broke out was the one about the prison. One media commentary noted that the four characters come from Sima Qian’s “Letter to Ren An”, where they originally meant being bound by outside rules. What Xu Fei meant by them was: no one locked me up; I trapped myself in this regret and couldn’t walk out.

But the most moving part of the show was actually the contrast. As viewers later pieced it together, when Xu Fei, her feelings pressed to the bottom, said this on the cliff off the cuff, Li Na not only didn’t understand it but froze on the spot, went back to her room to ask an AI, and ordered McDonald’s while she was at it. On the show she gave her full attention to eating and sighed, “Plain rice smells so good,” and the internet crowned her “the ceiling of thick skin” [钝感力天花板: 钝感力, “the power of bluntness”, a term borrowed from Japanese for not being easily hurt; 天花板, “ceiling”, net slang for the best of its kind].

One called her regret a prison of her own drawing; the other treated regret as a side dish for her rice. That contrast is the stock in the pot that made the construction take off: depth of feeling crashed into thick skin, the audience got poked once, then tickled once, and finally realized that a sentence could end like this.

## Why a name can be a full stop

Netizens quickly distilled the rule: put “Li Na” at the end of any everyday grumble, and the trivial thing is instantly wrapped in a wistful, regretful atmosphere, as if confiding in someone who isn’t there.

Some refused to believe it and tried other names. “Tan Weiwei” or “Li Yuchun” had no effect at all. Only the two syllables of “Li Na” close a sentence cleanly, like stamping a full stop on it, landing with a ring [掷地有声, “throw it on the ground and it rings”, an idiom for words that carry weight].

There is a linguistic mechanism behind this. A couple of days ago Shangguan News ran a dedicated linguistic reading: most proper nouns have no meaning; a name fixes what it refers to through an initial act of naming, and afterward carries meaning through historical, causal chains of culture. But once “Li Na” is used again and again at the end of sentences, it changes from a person’s name into a pure device of tone. It no longer points to a person; it points to a state: “the regret isn’t finished being said, but it doesn’t need to be.”

Put plainly, this is a name turning into a symbol. From Jia Junpeng to Li Na [贾君鹏, subject of a 2009 forum meme, “Jia Junpeng, your mom wants you home for dinner”], the past lives of name-memes were mapped out long ago: a name used over and over by millions finally becomes a container for feeling.

## From deep feeling to not wanting to go to work

The best part is how the construction drifted. It set out from deep feeling, then slid all the way into the office worker’s daily life:

“I’m up, Li Na.” “I’m off work, Li Na.” “I had a bowl of noodles, Li Na.”

“I don’t want to go to work, Li Na.” “I hate Mondays, Li Na.” “How did Saturday go by so fast, Li Na.”

The dignity of deep feeling lasted only a few days before office workers remade it into a sigh mark. Think about it and the evolution makes sense. The original line was funny because it voiced a private grievance through a public name; used in self-mockery about overtime, traffic, or weekends going too fast, it actually has more restraint than a string of exclamation marks.

It used to be that to complain about not wanting to go to work, you had to send a long “aaaaaargh” or a meltdown sticker. Now you need only four characters plus “Li Na,” and the grievance has a dignified place to land [the four characters are 不想上班, “don’t want to go to work”]. It isn’t as fierce as a resignation speech, nor as stifling as silence. It is simply an admission: I really don’t want to go to work, but I’m not planning to leave either.

One sentence says helplessness with a ring to it, and still leaves plenty of room.

## At the end of every meme is an ad

When a meme gets this big, brands are of course not going to let it go. A few days ago Miniso, Yili, Taobao, Yadea, Jinzai and a pile of other brands rushed out AI-generated videos to ride it: “Li Na, forgive Xu Fei.” Big screens in shopping malls showed “It was me who told on her! Forgive Xu Fei, Li Na.”

Some netizens thought it harmless, just playing with a meme. Lawyers warned otherwise: a short internet catchphrase lacks originality and isn’t protected by copyright law, but clipping footage straight from the show infringes the copyright in an audiovisual work; and brands using the names “Li Na” and “Xu Fei” directly in commercial advertising may infringe their right to their names.

Memes being co-opted is nothing new. From “you are my god” to “the gears of fate begin to turn,” every hot meme ends as ad copy. But the Li Na construction is a little different this time, because at heart it isn’t a phrase, it’s a full stop. To ride it, a brand has to turn the full stop back into a sentence, and the flavor changes at once.

Some ask: isn’t this just a meme frenzy? Is it worth all this? Even Shangguan News came out with a linguistic analysis; isn’t that over-reading?

But the fact that the Li Na construction has stayed hot for half a month is exactly what shows it’s more than a meme. Young people needing a name to serve as a full stop is itself worth chewing on.

## Feelings have gone too long without a way out

Think about where modern people’s feelings go. Anger has nowhere to go: a WeChat Moments post has to be sorted into friend groups. Regret has nowhere to go: a late-night Weibo post has to be deleted within seconds. Even a small feeling like “don’t want to go to work” has nowhere to go. Send it to a colleague and you seem precious; send it to your parents and you get a lecture; send it to your partner and they think you’re all negative energy.

So young people learned to check their feelings into a name. The two characters of Li Na make a perfect cloakroom: they point at no one, so they hurt no one; they come from a story far enough away, so they don’t give away your secrets; they land with a ring, so your grievance lands with a ring too.

**It’s not that the meme is so funny. It’s that the feelings have gone too long without a way out.**

It used to be called a sigh. Now it’s called Li Na.

## Ending:

Li Na later said on the show that she would not forgive, nor would she understand. Xu Fei’s “prison of my own drawing” never got an answer. But in young people’s hands those two characters have gained a whole new fate: from a regret that was never reconciled to a full stop for everyone.

Finish saying you don’t want to go to work, finish saying Monday, finish saying Saturday went too fast, and you still have to keep going to work. But at least the sentence has somewhere to land.

Not like a shout, not like silence.

Just a name, closing the sentence for you.
