# The End Point of Young People’s Weekends: A City Walk Through the Wet Market

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> Trial draft. The people and quotations in this piece are invented for demonstration and must be replaced with real interview samples before publication [to be verified].

Everyone knows that weekends are for seeing exhibitions, going camping, having a pour-over coffee.

But lately the people around me head out at 8 on Saturday morning — to the wet market. They carry a canvas tote, buy nothing in particular, and just wander. They call it a “wet-market City Walk” [“City Walk” is written in English in the original, a borrowed trend word].

Put plainly, it turns grocery shopping from a chore into a leisure activity.

Why the wet market? The practical reasons are simple: no ticket, a 30-yuan loop brings home a week of vegetables, and you grab breakfast on the way. The reasons of mood are a little more complicated: after five days at work, people need a place that is noisy but where nobody is hurrying them to get things done.

**But aren’t wet markets dirty, messy and grim?**

Xiao Yu, a designer in Chengdu born after 1995, doesn’t see it that way. The first time she went to the market by her home was because the delivery rider was an hour late. “The moment I walked in, the tofu auntie asked me, ‘Little sister, mapo or cold-dressed today?’ I froze. A whole week at work and nobody had asked me what I was eating.”

**But do young people even know how to buy vegetables?**

A-Kai, a programmer in Hangzhou, really doesn’t. His method is to follow the aunties. “Whichever tomato she picks, I pick the one next to it.” Three months on, he can tell free-range eggs from farm eggs: “Mostly you watch whether the stallholder looks guilty.”

**But isn’t this just taking photos for WeChat Moments somewhere new?**

Lin Lin, a graduate student in Guangzhou born after 2000, admits she went for the photos at first. Later she hardly took any, but she got friendly with the fish uncle. “Now he keeps the fish tails for me, because I once said they were tasty.”

**But how long can the enthusiasm last?**

Xiao Zhou, a social worker in Nanjing, has been going for a year. In her experience the novelty wears off in two months, and what stays is habit: on Saturday, first a bowl of duck-blood vermicelli soup, then up to the second floor to find the grandpa who sells only greens he grows himself. Grandpa doesn’t take mobile payment, so she always brings small change. “If I miss a week, it feels like I’ve skipped a check-in.” Once she was away on a work trip for two weeks; when she came back, Grandpa spoke first: “Girl, I kept last week’s greens for you. They’ve wilted. Have them for free.”

You see, there are no KPIs in the wet market, only “is the spinach tender today.”

ENDING:

**Young people haven’t fallen for the wet market because it’s stylish, but because here, someone remembers what you bought last week.**

After a loop, the canvas tote holds two spring onions, a block of tofu, and a bunch of baby greens just put out on the stall. Go home and cook a bowl of noodles, and the weekend has somewhere to rest.

Today’s topic: does the wet market near you have a stallholder who remembers you?
