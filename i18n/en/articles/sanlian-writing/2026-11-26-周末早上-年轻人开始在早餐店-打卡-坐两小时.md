# On Weekend Mornings, Young People Have Started “Checking In” at Breakfast Shops for Two Hours

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Everyone says young people on weekend mornings should be lazing in bed. Reach out and kill the alarm when it rings, sleep on until ten, and only then do you know how to relax.

But lately, at the breakfast shop downstairs, I’ve found the place full at eight-thirty. People order, take out laptops or stare at phone screens, and stay for two hours. The fried dough sticks are still hot, and the tables are already set out for work or job-hunting.

There’s no meeting room here, no fight for seats like at the library, and none of the lethargy of home that has you scrolling your phone until noon. Five yuan for a bowl of egg fried rice, plus two yuan for soy milk, buys a seat you can keep for two hours. For many people, that’s cheaper than renting a coworking space, and easier than forcing yourself to start work at home.

A-Lin. Tag: internet programmer, weekend breakfast-shop regular. He arrives at 8:15 on Saturday, orders a bowl of egg fried rice and two pork buns, and sets his laptop up in the corner by the window. “It’s quieter than the office and livelier than home,” he says. “At home I sit down and want to lie down. Here, I finish eating and I can start work.” Code scrolls across his screen; now and then he looks up at the bicycles outside, then goes back to typing. On the table is a half-drunk bottle of water, a grain of rice stuck to the rim of his bowl. He has asked for hot water twice, in no hurry to clear up.

Xiao Zhao. Tag: job-hunting, more than thirty CVs sent out. He doesn’t come for the food; he comes for a fixed seat he doesn’t have to fight for. Before he’s finished his soy milk, three interview invitations have popped up on his phone. “The library opens at seven on weekends, and the seats are gone by six-thirty. Here it’s five yuan a seat, and you don’t have to whisper.” He points at the woman at the next table, negotiating salary on the phone; against the background noise of dough sticks frying, her voice sounds especially real. She hangs up, sighs, and goes on chewing her dough stick.

Sister Wang. Tag: freelance illustrator, lives alone. She doesn’t cook much, and on weekend mornings she always comes for a jianbing [煎饼果子, a griddled savory crepe], bringing a tablet and sketching line drawings as she eats. “Alone at home it’s easy to scroll till noon, and then you regret it. Here, once you’ve eaten you have to start working, or the table goes to the next person.” The crepe sizzles on the griddle, her pen strokes across the page, as if pressing the start button on the weekend. An old man at the next table is reading the newspaper; now and then he glances at her tablet, then lowers his head and turns the page.

This isn’t some novel consumer trend. Young people have simply found a low-cost “third place.” No membership card, no booking ahead: just a steaming bowl of food. The owner won’t chase you out, and the street slowly wakes up outside the window. The owner walks past wiping tables; no one is hurried. Only near nine does she ask whether you’d like an extra egg. The breakfast shop at seven-thirty in the morning is one of the cheapest offices in the neighborhood.

Some say: isn’t this just freeloading on a seat? [白嫖, net slang for getting something for nothing.] Not necessarily. Buy food and you’re a customer; sit for two hours and no one turns nasty. That unspoken understanding is itself a kind of gentleness. More importantly, there’s no anxiety here about “what you should be doing with your weekend,” only everyone’s backs, each busy with their own thing, and the soy milk on the tables slowly going cold. No one asks how much you earn, no one asks about your progress; there’s just a bowl of food steaming in front of you.

It’s not because the breakfast is good that people come here; it’s because they need a place where they can sit down, be lightly looked at, and not have to explain themselves. A weekend begins with a bowl of wontons.
