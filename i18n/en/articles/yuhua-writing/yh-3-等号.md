# The Equals Sign

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

I died on a Wednesday afternoon, at the gate of the vegetable market, in the middle of haggling. A jin of celery: the man wanted four yuan, I said three-fifty, and when I had said it I went down. What the celery finally cost, I never found out.

The next day I arrived at the crematorium. There was a machine at the door, like the ones in a bank; you press it and it spits out a small slip of paper. I was number two hundred and thirty-seven.

The hall was full of people, all of them dead, all sitting very properly. On the wall was a screen of red characters, jumping from number to number. I had just sat down when the man beside me turned his head to look at me. It was Old Liu, who lives across the landing from me, third floor.

"Old Zhang. You came too," he said.

"I came," I said.

"What number are you?"

"Two hundred and thirty-seven."

"I'm two hundred and thirty-five, I'm ahead of you." He said it happily, as though being ahead of me in the queue were a piece of luck he had got. [Note: neighbours call them 老张 and 老刘, "Old Zhang" and "Old Liu", the ordinary way a man is addressed from middle age on.]

Old Liu owed me eight hundred yuan. He had borrowed it four years before. The first year he said he would pay it back next month; the second year, after New Year; the third year, when his son got paid; the fourth year, when he saw me he went around me, a long way around, out by the other gate of the compound. Now he could not go around me. Between us there was only a plastic armrest.

"That eight hundred yuan," I said.

"I've got it in mind," he said.

"You've had it in mind for four years."

"I have a good memory."

I asked when he had come. He said last Friday, his heart. He asked me, and I said heart too, at the vegetable market. He said what a coincidence, the two of us even died of the same complaint; a neighbour is a neighbour.

The screen jumped to two hundred and twenty-one. An old woman stood up and walked inwards, took two steps and came back, asking whether any of us had reading glasses, she could not see the signature she had to write. Nobody had any. Dead people don't bring things.

Old Liu said, "Old Zhang, it isn't that I won't pay you back. I have been meaning to pay you back all along."

"You've been meaning to for four years."

"Meaning to takes time too."

I said nothing. Then he took a piece of paper out of his pocket, his own number slip, turned it over, and on the back, in ballpoint, were a few characters: Owe Zhang Guoqiang eight hundred yuan. He said he had carried that slip for four years, through three pairs of trousers, and never lost it.

"You carry the slip, not the money," I said.

"The money won't carry. The slip carries," he said.

I thought about it, and what he said was true as well.

Number two hundred and thirty-five jumped up on the screen. Old Liu stood, pushed the paper into my hand and said, "You keep it. When you get to that side, you ask me for it."

"Where is that side?"

"I don't know either. Anyway, I'm going first. I'll wait for you over there. This time I won't go around."

Then he went in.

After a while the screen jumped to two hundred and thirty-six. Nobody stood up. After another while it jumped to two hundred and thirty-seven.

I folded Old Liu's paper and put it in my pocket, together with my own number slip. As I stood up I thought of that jin of celery. Three-fifty was too dear as well.
