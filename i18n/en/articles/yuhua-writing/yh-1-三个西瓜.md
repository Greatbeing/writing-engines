# Three Watermelons

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

When I met Chen Fusheng beside the county bus station, he was already sixty-eight, mending bicycle inner tubes under a parasol tree. He said he had bought three watermelons in his life, and then laughed at himself, showing four teeth.

He said the first watermelon was bought in 1979. That year he was twenty-three and just married to Guiying. Guiying said she wanted watermelon, so he walked twelve li to the town and spent four mao on the biggest one, and by the time he carried it back his arms were numb. [Note: a li is 500 metres; sums are given as in the original, where one yuan is ten mao and a jin is 500 grams.]

"What did you buy such a big one for?" Guiying said.

"A big one's a better deal," he said.

"Better deal my eye. We can't finish it, it'll go bad."

Guiying said that, but that night she ate half, hung the other half in a basket down the well, and ate that too the next day. Chen Fusheng said it was the most he had seen her eat at one time.

The second watermelon was bought in 1991. Their son Xiaojun had got into the county's No. 1 Middle School; only two children in the whole village got in. Chen Fusheng walked the twelve li again. This time it cost one yuan twenty, and again he picked the biggest. On the way he did the sums: watermelon had gone up three times over, while mending one tube still cost five mao. The account did not come out right. He bought it anyway.

Xiaojun held the watermelon and said, "Dad, it's so big, where did the money come from?"

"Sold half a sack of grain," he said.

"Then what do we eat at New Year?"

"New Year we'll see about when New Year comes."

That day the three of them sat on the doorsill eating watermelon, spitting the seeds on the ground, and the chickens ran up to peck at them. Guiying took the broom to drive them off; the chickens would not go, and she laughed and said these chickens are greedier than your father.

The third watermelon he told slowly.

In the summer of 2003, Xiaojun fell off a scaffold at a building site in the provincial capital. By the time Chen Fusheng got there, Xiaojun was already lying in an iron cabinet. The contractor gave him thirty-two thousand yuan wrapped in newspaper, and the newspaper had a film actress on it, laughing. Chen Fusheng put the money inside his coat, and at the fruit stall outside the hospital gate he bought one watermelon, for eight yuan.

He said to this day he did not know why he bought a watermelon that day.

He held the watermelon and rode the long-distance bus all night back. Guiying was waiting at the village entrance. She saw him holding a watermelon and no Xiaojun, and knew everything.

"The watermelons have gone up, haven't they?" Guiying said.

"Eight yuan," he said.

"Eight yuan," Guiying said. "That's sixteen jin of rice."

They cut that watermelon open, and neither of them ate any. They set it at the head of Xiaojun's bed and left it there until it rotted and filled the room with flies. Guiying drove the flies off and cursed, cursed the flies, cursed the watermelon, cursed him for throwing money around, and cursed nothing else.

Guiying went in 2010. Before she went she said she would like a mouthful of rice gruel. He went to the kitchen to boil it. When the gruel was ready, Guiying had no more need of it. He drank it himself, then washed the bowl and set it upside down on the stove.

After that he lived alone. In the morning he mended tubes; at noon he ate a bowl of noodles with one egg; at night he did not eat. He said when a man is old, eating at night keeps him awake, and not eating keeps him awake too, so he does not eat, and saves a meal.

He put Xiaojun's thirty-two thousand yuan into the credit cooperative and kept the passbook under Guiying's pillow. In more than ten years he drew on it twice: once for Guiying's treatment, once to buy Guiying's coffin. He said the rest he would not touch; it was for buying himself a better urn. "It can't be worse than Guiying's. If it's worse she'll curse me."

Every summer the watermelon three-wheeler drove up to the bus station gate and the loudspeaker called, "Mealy and sweet, no money if it isn't sweet!" He kept his head down and mended tubes, finished one, started another. The seller knew him and asked whether he wanted one. He said no. The seller said, you say no every day. He said, every day what I say is true.

I asked whether he still bought watermelons after that.

He pressed a mended tube into the basin of water and looked at it. No bubbles.

"No more," he said. "A man on his own buying a watermelon, it's too big. Can't finish it, it'll go bad."

Then he laughed again and said this was what Guiying had said, she had said it for more than forty years, and he had finally taken it in.

I wanted to say something. He had already bent his head to look for the next tube.

A student came to collect his bicycle. He took three yuan, dropped it into the tin box, and it rang once.

My bicycle was done too.
