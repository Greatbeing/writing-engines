# Read

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> Subject: the office worker's anxiety over being left on read | Runtime: about 4 minutes | Venue: open mic
> Atom chain: Observational Opening → Self-Deprecation → Rule of Three → Escalation → Act-out → Mismatched Analogy → Callback

Have you noticed that the chat apps have this feature now. It's called "Read."

You send the message. They take a look. And two little words appear on your side: Read.

Those two words are very small.

*(Pause)*

But they are very loud.

Me, I have excellent mental toughness.

I can survive overtime. I can survive a pay cut. I can survive my mother asking what I make.

I cannot survive "Read."

Last Friday night I sent my boss a proposal.

Sent at 8:03.

At 8:04 — Read.

And then there was no then.

*(Pause)*

Do you know what that one minute means?

It means he looked at it.

He looked at it, and he chose silence.

That's crueler than yelling at me.

Yelling means the proposal still has a chance.

Silence — does that mean I still have a chance?

That night I did three things.

First, I read the proposal over again.

Second, I read the chat history over again.

Third, I opened my résumé.

*(Pause)*

That's the kind of crisis-conscious person I am.

Then I started analyzing.

I screenshotted that "Read" and sent it to three friends.

The first one said, he's probably busy.

The second one said, he's probably thinking.

The third one said, you sent it at eight on a Friday night?

*(Pause)*

Then you had it coming.

I thought he was right. I couldn't accept it.

So I began to extrapolate.

The boss hasn't replied, which means he's dissatisfied.

Dissatisfied, which means there's a meeting on Monday.

A meeting, which means in front of everybody.

In front of everybody, which means somebody will be screen-recording.

Screen-recorded, which means it reaches the other departments.

The other departments, which means even the lady in the canteen downstairs will hear about it.

*(as the canteen lady, ladling out food, then a beat)*

"So you're the one. The 8:04."

*(back to normal voice)*

"Here, have an extra scoop. You won't be eating here much longer."

One in the morning, still awake.

I stare at that "Read." It stares back at me.

*(low voice, to the phone)*

"Say something. Go on."

*(the phone's voice, cool)*

"I did say something. I said: Read."

And I've realized that being left on read is exactly like waiting your turn at a hospital.

You get a number. The nurse has seen you.

She just won't call you.

And you don't dare leave.

Leave, and your number gets called and you're gone.

Monday morning I got to the office at nine.

Normally I get there at 9:50.

The boss walks in, sees me, nods.

I think: here it comes.

He comes over and says, "That proposal—"

*(Pause)*

"I was driving Friday night. Opened it, the screen locked. Haven't read it yet."

*(Pause)*

I spent an entire weekend anxious about a lock screen.

I was on the third draft of my résumé.

So I got smart.

I turned my own read receipts off.

Now when people message me, they can't see whether I've read it.

At last I'm free.

*(Pause)*

Except last week I sent my boss a proposal, and he'd turned his off too.

Now I can't even see "Read."

And I've started to miss those two little words.

At least back then, at 8:04, I knew somebody had looked at me. [Note: 已读, "read," is the two-character read-receipt tag in Chinese chat apps, so the "two little words" are literally two characters; 已读不回, "read but not replied," is the everyday name for this anxiety.]

*(Pause)*

Thank you. I'm on the fourth draft of my résumé now — happy to connect.
