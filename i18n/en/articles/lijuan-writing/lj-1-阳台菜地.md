# The Vegetable Plot on the Balcony

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

My mother has been growing vegetables on the balcony for three years now.

The balcony is four square metres, facing south. She has lined it with eleven foam boxes, all begged from the fruit shop downstairs — [泡沫箱: free foam crates, the standard planter of a Chinese balcony garden; the shops are glad to be rid of them]. The sides of the boxes still read “Red-Fleshed Dragon Fruit,” so our scallions grow out of dragon fruit and our garlic sprouts out of “Premium Cherries,” and only one chili pepper lives in a proper pot. I bought that pot. She thought it was too expensive, and she still brings it up.

I don't really understand growing vegetables. All I know is that vegetables come from the supermarket, already washed, packed in a plastic box, four and a half yuan for one box. My mother says that if a person like me were dropped in the countryside, three days of hunger would sort me out. I thought about it, decided she was right, and did not argue.

In spring she sows bok choy seed. The seeds are tiny and black, as if someone had spilled sesame over the soil. She sows them solemnly, bent over, rolling them out one by one between her fingers, counting aloud as she goes. Halfway through she loses the count and starts again from the beginning. Watching from the side, I think this is more serious than my whole day at work.

Five or six days later, a patch of green pushes up through the soil. A real patch, packed so tight the seedlings can hardly stand. My mother squats there looking at them for a long time and says, “They're all mine.” Then she is a little embarrassed and adds, “Too crowded. Half of them will have to come out.”

But she cannot bear to pull a single one.

Later, when the bok choy is as big as a palm, she finally makes up her mind, pulls a small bunch, and stir-fries one plate. There are maybe seven or eight stalks on it. She sets the plate in the middle of the table, next to a big bowl of pork ribs from the supermarket, and keeps pushing it toward me, watching me eat, asking, “Sweeter than the ones outside?”

I say yes. I can't actually tell. But the way she looks, I feel that saying I couldn't tell would be unfair to those four square metres.

Summer is the liveliest. The chilies set fruit; the tomatoes climb their string all the way to the laundry pole and hang there among my shirts. In the morning, taking in the washing, I have to ask the tomato's arm out of my sleeve first. There are ants too, up from who knows which floor, marching in file along the edge of the foam boxes. My mother can do nothing about them, so she reasons with them: “This is my house. Go somewhere else.” The ants don't listen. They keep walking.

One evening I came home from work and saw her sitting alone on a low stool, spray bottle in hand, not watering anything, just sitting. The sun was going down behind the building opposite, and the whole row of windows was lighting up orange-red, one square at a time. Below, someone was walking a dog; cars were hunting for parking spaces; far off, a construction site was making its noise. Across the way, several hundred households, several hundred balconies, quilts airing, cardboard boxes stacked, mops leaning against the rail. Only our one square had a little scrub of green coming out of it, all anyhow.

At that moment I suddenly felt that my mother and her scallions, in a city this big, were very, very small. So small that if you looked up from the street below, you would never find them.

But they don't know it. The scallions keep shooting up, the tomatoes keep climbing out, and my mother keeps getting up at six every morning — to look at them first, and then to put the kettle on.

In autumn she picks the last crop of chilies: nineteen of them, some red, some green. She spreads them on newspaper and counts them one by one, twice, and both times it comes to nineteen. She is very pleased, and says that next year she will plant two more boxes.

I say there is no room left on the balcony.

She looks at my treadmill and says nothing.
