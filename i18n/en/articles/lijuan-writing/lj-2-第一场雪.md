# The First Snow

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

At six forty in the morning I pulled back the curtain, and outside was white.

I stood there blank for about ten seconds, then went to tell my mother. She was boiling thin dried noodles in the kitchen, didn't turn her head, and said, “So it snows and you don't go to work?”

I said no, I was only telling her.

She said she had seen it at six; there was a finger's depth on the car roofs downstairs — did it need saying?

So I went and dressed myself. Long johns, sweater, down jacket, scarf, hat. When I was done I stood in front of the mirror like a garbage bag packed too full. My mother glanced over and said, “You're off to the South Pole.”

Outside the building door the snow was still coming down. The flakes were small, one grain at a time, not the goose feathers the books describe — more like someone up there sifting flour, and not sifting very carefully. A layer was already on the ground; it creaked under my feet. I took a few steps and looked back: a string of footprints behind me, crooked, like a man who had been drinking. And I had not drunk anything.

Downstairs the electric scooters had all turned into white steamed buns, a dozen of them in a row, perfectly neat. On one seat only, someone had written a character with a finger: 王 [Wang, four strokes, the commonest surname in China — a name, a claim, or a joke, all of them at once]. I don't know who wrote it, or who it was written for.

Old Li, the guard at the gate of the compound, was sweeping snow. He swept slowly: one sweep, a pause, a look at the sky. The sky kept sifting down; where he had swept, it was white again after a while. I thought his work could never be finished, and that it was much like my own.

The bus stop was eight hundred metres away. Usually I walk it in nine minutes; that day it took sixteen. On one downhill stretch I went down sideways, inching, both hands spread out, like a duck that wants to take off and hasn't the nerve. Beside me a schoolboy with his backpack slid straight down the slope, and slid beautifully. At the bottom he turned and looked back at me.

I pretended to be looking at my phone.

The bus was twelve minutes late. Twenty-odd people crowded onto the platform, all with their necks pulled in, nobody speaking, their breath rising in puffs. Snow fell on every shoulder, on hats, on one man's eyebrows. The man didn't wipe it off; he just stood there with white eyebrows, as if he had aged ten years all at once.

The bus came at last. I squeezed on and leaned against the window. It climbed slowly onto the elevated road; outside, the whole city was white. The tall buildings were white, the bridges were white, and the far sprawl of factory sheds whose names I have never known was white too. The cars on the road drove very carefully, their red taillights in strings, creeping along.

A city this big, covered overnight by snow, covered so flat, so unreasonably. The whole busload of us sat inside it, small as a few grains of dust in the snow.

But dust still has to go to work.

At the office the section chief was standing by the lift. He looked at me and said, “There's still snow on your hat.”

I reached up and pinched off a little; it had already melted to water and was running down my fingers.

I shook it onto the floor.
