# Grandmother

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> Demonstration draft. The material is invented; for testing only.

Grandmother — 外婆, wàipó, my mother's mother, a different word in Chinese from the father's mother — is eighty-one this year.

Her hearing isn't good; she keeps the television at volume thirty-eight [loud enough to carry from the sitting room to the kitchen]. I say it's too loud. She says, “You're young. Put up with it.”

Every morning at five thirty she gets up to water the flowers. There are seven pots on the balcony; three of them died long ago, and she waters them all the same. I say those three are dead. She says, “How do you know? Did you ask them?”

I hadn't asked them, so I said nothing more.

Lately she has learned to take photographs with her phone. She takes a great many: the dishes, the clouds, the cat downstairs, me. When she photographs me she always gets half a head, the other half outside the frame. She looks at the result with satisfaction and says, “Makes your face look smaller.”

Last month she called me over and solemnly handed me a task: teach her to send voice messages. I taught her for twenty minutes. She got it; her first message went to my uncle, and she held the button and talked for forty seconds, then let go and looked — the whole thing was her asking, “Is it recording?”

My uncle replied with one character: “Mom?” [舅舅, the mother's brother; Chinese keeps the exact relationship in the word, so a single character from him carries the whole of it.]

She laughed until the tears came, handed the phone back to me, and said, “Look at your uncle. Silly.”

The truth is I don't quite understand how it works myself. Half the functions on my own phone I can't use, but in front of Grandmother I am the expert.

After dinner she goes downstairs to walk two laps. She walks slowly, step by step, and when she passes that cat she stops, bends down, and says a few words to it. What she says I can't make out. The cat takes no notice of her either.

She straightens up, pats the dust off her trousers, and walks on.
