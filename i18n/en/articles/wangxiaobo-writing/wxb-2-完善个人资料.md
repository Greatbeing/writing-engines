# On the Completeness of One's Personal Profile

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

A few days ago I downloaded an app for keeping accounts, meaning to track where my money goes each month. I opened it, and the first screen was not the accounts at all but a progress bar, with the words: Your profile is 20% complete. A more complete profile gives you better service. I looked at it for a while, felt that the twenty per cent referred to me, and was mildly embarrassed.

So I began completing it. I entered my birthday; the bar rose to thirty. Occupation: forty-five. Education and income bracket: seventy. The remaining thirty per cent required me to upload a photograph, link my contacts, and choose three interest tags. I hesitated a long time over the "interests" column, because my interest is not filling in forms, and that was not among the options.

I have a slow brain and cannot see why an accounting app needs to know my education, so I did the honest thing and reasoned it out. If the more complete the profile, the better the service, then a person who has filled in one hundred per cent ought to receive the best service of all. By that reasoning, the best user would enter his height, his blood type, and the name of his first love. Push it further, and the person the app knows best is no longer me but the progress bar; and once it knows me better than I know myself, keeping the accounts need not be my job at all — it will tell me where this month's money went, and incidentally where next month's money ought to go. Push it to the end, and a person whose profile is one hundred per cent complete is probably a person who no longer has to make any decision of his own.

This reminds me of something from physics class: measurement changes the thing measured. Put a thermometer into a small cup of water and the water gives up a little of its heat to the thermometer. The app measures me with a form, and while it measures, I slowly grow into the shape of the form: the occupation column takes only one value, the interests only three, and more are not accepted.

I have also known a cat. Its owner bought it a collar with a number and a chip, which recorded how many steps it took each day and how many hours it slept. After two days it crawled under the bed and would not come out however it was called. Later it wore the collar through on something and came out to eat as though nothing had happened. In terms of data, that cat's profile has been incomplete ever since; from the cat's point of view, it probably considers itself complete.

I have no ringing opinion on the matter. Some say this is a question of privacy and one should cry out about it; others say it is technological progress and one should embrace it. Both of these are said very loudly, and I cannot squeeze in between them. I only want to sit to one side and explain clearly the process by which the bar went from twenty to seventy.

My objection to filling in profiles is not chiefly that I am afraid of anything; it is that it is so dull. The interesting things about a person — why he wants a fried pancake at midnight [Note: 煎饼, jianbing: a cheap street pancake, the standard late-night craving], why he keeps accounts for three days and then gives up — not one of them is in the form. The form contains only the dullest parts, and it insists that these are "the complete you."

So my profile remains at seventy per cent to this day. The other thirty I intend to keep for myself, which counts as a kind of completion too. As for the accounting app, I have not recorded a single entry, and it probably knows that better than I do.
