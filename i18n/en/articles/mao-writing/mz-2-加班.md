# Overtime: First Tell Which Kind It Is

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

How should a young person treat overtime? Many meet the question every day, and few sit down and think it through.

My view is this: do not be in a hurry to say whether overtime is good or bad. Overtime is not one thing; it has to be looked at separately. Sorted by cause, there are roughly three kinds.

The first kind: there really is a great deal to do. The project has reached its gate, the client wants the goods tomorrow, everyone stays up two nights together and finishes the work. This overtime has a beginning and an end [Note: 有头有尾, "with a head and a tail," and so it is complete], and when it is over you feel settled.

The second kind: poor arrangement. Three meetings in the daytime, not one real task touched, and the work starts after hours. It is not that there is much to do; the day was eaten away in pieces. This overtime burns the night to patch the holes of the day.

The third kind: doing it for people to see. The boss has not left, so you are embarrassed to leave; you sit at your desk scrolling on your phone, and clock out at nine. In this overtime the person is there and the work is not.

Three kinds of overtime, much the same look: a light on at night. But their nature is very different. The first can be accepted, the second has to be changed, the third should be cut down.

Someone will say: jobs are so hard to find now, what standing do I have to pick and choose? If the boss says work late, work late.

There is sense in that. A person new to the work who does a little more also learns faster; that is the good side. But the other side must be seen too: the body is your own, and a year of burning it out takes ten years to restore. Both sides must be seen, but the main question is not whether to work overtime; it is why. Once the cause is clear, you know whether to endure it, to change it, or to leave.

As the saying goes, 磨刀不误砍柴工 [Note: "sharpening the axe does not delay the woodcutting"]. Sharpen the knife by day, and there is less wood to cut at night.

I know a young woman who designs. When she first joined she too worked until ten every night. Then she did one small thing: every morning at work she wrote in a notebook the three things that had to be finished that day, and carried the notebook into meetings. Three months later her overtime had gone from five days a week to one. She did no less work, and she was picked for the annual award besides.

Her method is not complicated. Anyone can learn it.

There is another deviation to guard against. Some people hear that overtime must be sorted out, and count everything as the third kind; even at the gate itself they will not stay one minute longer. That is wrong too. Not staying up when the night is needed, letting the project fail: in the end it is you who loses. Sorting is for knowing where you stand, not for finding excuses.

So how is it to be told apart? I see two tests. First, is there a specific piece of work? If you can say what must be delivered tonight, it is real overtime. Second, how was the day spent? If the day was cut to pieces by meetings and messages, fix the day first.

So before the next overtime, we might ask ourselves one question: this hour tonight, which kind is it? If it is the first, work with an easy mind. If it is the second, write down those three things tomorrow morning. If it is the third, pack up and go home on time.
