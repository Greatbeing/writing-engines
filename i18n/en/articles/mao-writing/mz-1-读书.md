# Reading: Prescribe to the Illness

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

What does it take to say you have really understood a book? People who read every day may never have sat down and thought it through.

Look first at two ways of reading.

The first is the "copying school." The book comes home, the notebook opens, and you copy it out page by page. The key passages are underlined in three colors: red for concepts, blue for examples, green for the lines worth quoting [Note: 金句, literally "golden sentences," the lines that sound good enough to be repeated aloud]. Three months of this and two notebooks are full. Ask what the book is about, and he opens the notebook and reads it to you. Close the notebook, and he cannot say.

The second is the "flipping school." A book in hand, he reads the contents page, the opening, the ending, gets through it in half an hour, and tells everyone he has read it. Read it he has, but it was 走马看花 [Note: "viewing flowers from a galloping horse," that is, looking without looking closely]. How many flowers were open, and in what color, he never saw.

The two ways look different; the fault is one: the book is the book, I am I, and the two never meet.

So what would it mean for them to meet? I see at least three conditions.

First, read with a question in hand. A doctor asks where it hurts before prescribing; a reader should ask himself what he is missing. When work has jammed, go and read the books that can loosen that knot. With a question in the mind, the eye will stop on the page. Without one, every page is the same flat ground.

Second, say it in your own words. Finish a chapter, close the book, try telling it to your family. If you can tell it, you understood; if you cannot, you did not; if you stumble through it, you understood half. The method is clumsy, and it works.

Third, take it to a real task. The book says a plan should leave itself room [Note: 余地, "leeway," room to spare]. Next week, schedule one week that way. If it works, the principle has grown into you; if it fails, go back to the book, and this time you will read what you did not see the first time.

At this point someone will say: read like that and you will not finish many books in a year. It is too slow.

There is half a truth in that. Reading widely has its good side: the horizon widens. Reading closely has its good side too: the roots go deep. You want both, but not with equal force. The main problem is not that you read little; it is that you do not digest what you read. It is like eating: eat however much, and if you do not digest it, the body puts on no flesh. So skimming may be fast; close reading must be slow. Three to five books read closely and thirty to fifty skimmed in a year is manageable.

There is another deviation to guard against. Some people hear the word "use" and stop reading the book itself. They read a few reviews, remember a few terms, and go about talking. That is wrong too. "Use" without reading is water without a source [Note: 无源之水, "water with no source," and so it cannot last]. Reviews are worth reading, but they are food someone else has chewed; they can tide you over, they cannot be the meal. When a book is finished, leave at least one sentence of your own in the notebook, even if it is clumsily written.

As the saying goes, 磨刀不误砍柴工 [Note: "sharpening the axe does not delay the woodcutting"]. Read a little slower, and you will use it faster; use it once, and the next reading goes faster still.

Many people read. Not so many really understand. It is not because books are hard; it is because the way of reading is wrong.

The book at hand: try it tonight. Write one question on the flyleaf, then open to page one. Finish a chapter, and tell one person. Finish the book, and put one thing to the test.

Three things, none of them hard. The hard part is keeping at it.
