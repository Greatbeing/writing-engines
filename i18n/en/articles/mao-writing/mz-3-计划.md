# Why Plans Don't Hold

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

You make a plan and cannot keep it up. Where is the fault? Many people take it for weak willpower. I think the trouble is mostly not in the will but in the plan itself.

As the saying goes, 一口吃不成胖子 [Note: "you cannot get fat on one mouthful"]. Yet many people make plans as though one mouthful would do it.

Look first at a common way of going about it. At the turn of the year you buy a new notebook and fill the first page: up at six every day, run five kilometers, memorize fifty words, read for an hour, review the evening. You finish writing, excited, feeling that the new year will be different from here on. The first week you keep to it; the second week you miss two days; by the third week the notebook is under the drawer.

This kind of plan I call the "new year plan." It has three faults: too much, too full, too far. Five things stuffed into one day, not one of them leaving any room, and the target set a year away. People are not machines; one day of overtime, one illness, and the whole table collapses. And when the table collapses the person loses heart and throws the whole thing away.

So how is a plan to hold? I see three ways.

First, fewer. Grasp one thing at a time. If you want to run, then for now just run; put the rest behind.

Second, smaller. Cut the thing down to a size you can do even when you do not want to. Five kilometers is hard, so first put on your shoes and go out; ten minutes of walking counts.

Third, nearer. Set the target for this week, not for this year. When the week is done, put a tick in the notebook, and add a little next week.

Someone will say: that is too slow. What will you accomplish in a year? There is half a truth in it. Fast has its advantages, but fast cannot be kept up, which is the same as not moving at all. Slower, and always moving, and in a year you can go a long way.

There is another deviation to guard against. Some people hear "small" and shrink it to nothing; the plan is left with one line, "as much as possible." As much as possible means not doing it. Small is so that it can be done, not so that it need not be. However small the step, write down which day and how much.

And what if the chain breaks? Then join it again. Miss a day, and the next day carry on as usual: do not make it up, and do not punish yourself. A plan need not fear a break; what it fears is not being joined again after the break.

Few in number, small in step, near in sight.

This weekend, do one thing: dig out the old notebook, keep one line, cross out all the rest. Then set the smallest action you could manage next Monday, and write it beside that line. On Monday night, look at it again and see whether it was done.
