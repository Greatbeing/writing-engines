# Let the Spirit of Craftsmanship Shine in the New Era

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

A country's confidence is never written only in grand blueprints; it is also written on tens of millions of pairs of working hands. From a single screw to a giant ship, from a length of wire to a power grid, every leap in manufacturing is carried out process by process and lifted by one generation of workers after another. The new era needs the spirit of craftsmanship more than ever; the stage of development we stand in decides it.

Standing at a new starting point and looking back, the road behind is plain to see; looking ahead, the journey is still wide open. In the past, the question we solved was "whether we have it"; today, we must answer "whether it is good" and "whether it is fine." Going from big to strong, what we cross is not only scale but quality; what we compete on is not only speed but craft.

## Coming from the Depths of History

The spirit of craftsmanship runs long and deep in Chinese civilisation — 源远流长 [yuán yuǎn liú cháng, "a source far back, a stream running long"]. More than two thousand years ago, Li Bing and his son built Dujiangyan on the Min River, and it still irrigates the Chengdu Plain today. That a single project serves for two thousand years rests on reverence for natural law and a stubborn exactness about detail. The ancient classics' records of the hundred crafts say it again and again: to be exact in one thing and devoted to one trade is a character the Chinese have had since antiquity.

After the founding of New China, group after group of industrial workers, in bare-bones workshops, hand-scraped and hand-fitted, tested and tested again, and turned out one "proud-of-ourselves" product after another. Since reform and opening, more skilled workers have stepped onto the stage of great-power manufacturing. The times change, the tools change; that devotion to "a good piece of work" has not changed.

## Seeing an Era from One Workshop

In an old machine-tool plant, a veteran master runs his fingers over a part just off the line, narrows his eyes and says: "A hair off, and it isn't work." His "hair" is one hundredth of a millimetre. Over the decades his apprentices have come in crop after crop; some are now team leaders, some technical mainstays at new plants. In his toolbox there still sits an old file, worn bright with use.

Such figures are not rare on today's production lines, in laboratories, on construction sites. Someone recalibrates a high-speed-rail bogie frame again and again; someone fights over microns on a chip line; someone squats a whole day before a bridge weld. One ordinary name after another adds up to the firmest foundation of a manufacturing power.

## From One Process to a Nation

精益求精 [jīng yì qiú jīng, "refining the already refined"], 久久为功 [jiǔ jiǔ wéi gōng, "long effort that accumulates"] — this is the character of the craftsman, and of a nation too. A screw turned true, and a machine runs steady; a machine running steady, and a line runs fast; ten thousand lines running fast, and a country's manufacturing stands up. The value of the spirit of craftsmanship lies here: turning "close enough" into "not a hair off," and one person's craft into a nation's strength.

Today's craftsmen do not only swing hammers and work files; they must also understand programming and debug. Digitalisation and smart manufacturing have changed how work is done, but not the standard of "doing the thing well." The more precise the machine, the more it needs a more precise person; the more advanced the technology, the more it needs a steadier heart.

In some new smart factories, processes once judged by feel have gone to sensors and algorithms. But how to set the parameters, how to judge an anomaly, which link caps the yield — the final call still goes to the old masters who know the equipment's temper. They write decades of experience into process documents, then teach it line by line to young people just out of school. Experience flows, craft upgrades, and the spirit finds a new carrier. New technology is not the craftsman's rival but his new tool; whoever uses that tool well is the true craftsman of the new era.

## Facing the Weight of the Cold Bench

We must also see that not many young people will sit on the 冷板凳 [lěng bǎn dèng, "the cold bench" — the long, unglamorous apprenticeship], and "slow work" often does not turn into quick money. Some places prize credentials over skill, some firms prize using people over training them, and the path for skilled workers is still not wide enough. This cannot be made up for by individual moral resolve alone. It needs the people who do solid work to get better returns and greater respect.

To give skill a price and the craftsman dignity, we need to open the channels of assessment, pay and promotion; match vocational education to what industry needs; and build a public mood that "holds skill in esteem and respects labour." When a senior technician's income and sense of achievement are enough to set a mind at ease, 择一事终一生 [zé yī shì zhōng yī shēng, "choose one thing, stay with it for life"] stops being only a slogan.

## Do Every Process Well

With skill in the hands, the heart has confidence; with roots underfoot, the road ahead has direction. The exam paper of the new era is laid out, and those answering it are everyone who insists on being exact at their post.

Do every process well, make every product fine, pass every craft on — and we can write, on the exam paper of our times, the answer that belongs to this generation.
