# A Three-Li Mountain Path, Leading to the Distance Beyond the Mountains

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Shangqing Township, in the Longhu Mountain scenic area of Yingtan, Jiangxi, has a three-li mountain path. According to reports, the track is pitted and uneven — 坑坑洼洼 [kēng kēng wā wā, "full of pits and hollows"] — and even a healthy person needs more than half an hour to walk it. The rural teacher Li Yucai walked it with a thermos of hot water in one hand and books in the other, and "kept at it for more than thirty years." [1] In 1978 he took on the teaching at this mountain school point in Shangli Village and became its only community-run teacher; in 2004 he developed stomach cancer, four-fifths of his stomach was removed, and after leaving hospital he went back to the podium. [1]

One man walked a mountain path for more than thirty years, and what he measured was the weight of rural education. The bottom line of educational equity lies in the countryside, and the ground colour of rural education lies with its teachers. One end of the path joins a classroom; the other joins the possibility that children in the mountains will get somewhere. Whoever holds this road holds the most basic storey of the country's educational building.

Numbers best show the weight of that persistence. Reports show that Li Yucai has taught more than 300 students from five village groups in his home area, and the classes he taught often ranked near the top in the township — 名列前茅 [míng liè qián máo, "name among the front rows of rushes"]. [1] Across the country, according to a 2025 People's Daily report, the special-post teacher programme for compulsory-education schools in rural areas has run for twenty years, and millions of rural teachers, special-post teachers among them, have taken root in the vast countryside. [2] The three hundred-odd students of one school point flow into the tens of millions of families held up by millions of rural teachers.

Detail best reveals the weight of persistence. The report records that when his stomach hurt during class, he pressed it against the corner of the desk, doing his utmost to hide the pain and keep teaching; when it struck at night while he was marking homework, he knelt on both knees and braced his stomach against the edge of the bed, and finished the marking and the lesson preparation. [1] On a heavy-snow day in the winter of 2005 his stomach hurt so badly that he did not close his eyes for two days and two nights, and he still walked into the classroom on a wooden stick. [1] When a child in the village could not pay the school fee, he scrimped on his own food and clothing to pay it for the child — 省吃俭用 [shěng chī jiǎn yòng, "skimping on food and clothing"] — and of the fees he had advanced he said, "I can't remember exactly how much." [1] Details like these need no embellishment; they carry their weight on their own.

One mountain path, and what walks out of it is one man's persistence; three hundred-odd students, and what they carry on is one mountain village's hope; millions of rural teachers, and what they hold up is the foundation of rural revitalisation. Persistence is precious because it turns a job into a cause, and a choice made at one moment into a responsibility for a lifetime.

We must also see the other side of that persistence: its cost. In September 2013 Li Yucai collapsed at the podium and was diagnosed with pancreatitis brought on by long-term overwork and irregular eating; the following year he was hospitalised and operated on again after a recurrence. [1] Leaders from the education department urged him to rest, and his answer was: "If I take leave, what happens to those children?" [1] That a sick teacher dares not ask for leave has its root in there being no one behind him to take over. Persistence deserves a salute, but it cannot rest on one person's body alone — 孤军奋战 [gū jūn fèn zhàn, "one lone army fighting on"] is not a model to be preserved. Only by filling the teacher shortage in rural schools, setting up stable substitute and rotation mechanisms, and making sure rural teachers can see a doctor when ill and rest when tired, can those who persist stop fighting alone.

Only if those who persist do not lose out will persistence have successors. More warmth in pay, more channels for development, more respect in society — when good policy lands in one school point after another, the three-li mountain path will no longer be one man's long march.

Long as the mountain path is, it can measure a teacher's first intention; small as the podium is, it can hold up a mountain village's future. The best way to salute those who persist is to make more people willing to take up that piece of chalk.
