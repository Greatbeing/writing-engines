# Jensen Huang: The Eleven Days He Was Absent

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

On November 23, Nvidia's board announced that it had restored Jensen Huang to the posts of president and chief executive. On the first trading day after the announcement, Nvidia's share price closed up 31.48 percent at $165.05 — a one-day increase in market value of about $952 billion, a record single-day gain for a listed American company.

In the eleven days before that, the company had been through the most violent turmoil of its thirty-three years. After an October high, the share price at one point fell nearly 50 percent.

The most valuable company in the world discovered, in eleven days, that its moat was not in chips. It was in the man in the leather jacket.

## I. Eleven Days

It began on November 8. That day, Huang released a statement on Nvidia's website: he admitted to infidelity toward his wife, apologised to his family, his employees and his shareholders, and said he took full responsibility.

Within a week the matter did not stop. Several outlets reported, one after another, on relationships with other people, and the board opened an independent investigation.

On the morning of November 12 the announcement landed: Huang resigned as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. Thirty-three years, three titles, set down at once. Chief financial officer Colette Kress became interim CEO, and Harvey Jones, a long-serving director, was appointed the company's first chairman.

Between November 5 and November 12, Nvidia's shares fell more than 22 percent.

Then the market began to price a single thing: what this company is worth without him.

On November 23 the board voted unanimously to restore Huang to his posts. The statement said he had shown candour, and that the board was satisfied with his leadership.

Huang wrote in his statement: "I am honoured to return to Nvidia, and at the opening of a new industrial revolution, to lead one of the most influential technology companies in the world — I cannot wait to get back to work."

On Monday the share price gave its answer: 31.48 percent. $952 billion in a single day.

## II. Thirty Years

Huang was born in Taiwan in 1963. At nine, he and his older brother were put on a plane to the United States by their parents. Before they left, their mother picked ten words at random out of a dictionary every day and made the two boys memorise them — she did not speak English herself.

In 1993, Huang, then thirty, and two partners settled on a business plan at a Denny's in San Jose, put in $40,000 of their own money, and founded Nvidia. The name comes from the Roman goddess of envy.

The start was a disaster. The first chip, the NV1, was out of step with the industry's technical standards; the product would not sell; the company cut staff until thirty people remained, and there was a month's payroll in the account. "We were thirty days away from closing." At the end of his rope, Sega of Japan sent a $7 million order, and the company lived. In 1997 the RIVA 128 succeeded and steadied the foundation. In 1999 Nvidia listed on Nasdaq and the GeForce 256 appeared — and the idea of the GPU entered the world.

In 2006 he made a decision nobody understood at the time: he launched CUDA. Let the graphics card do more than draw; let it compute. For the ten years after that, CUDA was the quietest line in Nvidia's financial statements — vast investment, almost no revenue. Analysts asked, over and over: why keep a thing that earns nothing?

In 2012 a laboratory at the University of Toronto trained AlexNet on two Nvidia graphics cards and crushed every rival in the image-recognition contest. The age of deep learning began with those two cards.

In 2023 generative AI broke open. The whole world discovered that compute was the hard currency of this revolution. Nvidia's revenue for fiscal 2026 was $215.9 billion, up 65 percent year on year; data-centre revenue for a single quarter was $62.3 billion.

A ten-year bet, paid out in the eleventh year.

## III. Thirty Days

"We were thirty days away from closing." That sentence travelled from the survival fact of 1993 into a crisis culture that ran through thirty-three years.

People thought he 赢麻了 — that he had won so hard there was nothing left to win [internet slang, kept: "won to the point of numbness"]. He went on assuming the company would fold tomorrow.

In meeting rooms, what he kept talking about was "mission", and the world ten years out — a computing architecture built for CREATIVITY, one that paved the way for AI, and whose return was always invisible in the quarter's numbers. On the GTC stage he held up a new chip, black leather jacket, the company logo tattooed on his left arm. At the annual party he danced yangge and handed out red envelopes.

The other side of all that spotlight is the child who graduated from a school for problem kids: by day he taught his roommate to read, by night he did a hundred push-ups; two years later the family emigrated, he became a top student, courted the girl in his class, and declared that he would be a CEO before he turned thirty.

He delivered. Then, thirty-three years on, because of one slip, he fell briefly from the peak.

People looked at him again. Not because of the chips this time.

## IV. Back on Stage

Monday's close: $165.05. A record one-day gain in market value.

The board said that the reinstatement was in the best interest of shareholders, employees, partners and customers. His statement had one core line: "I cannot wait to get back to work."

The stage is still there. The leather jacket is still there. At the next GTC he will stand in the light holding up a new chip — as he has for thirty-three years.

A generation's computing revolution is still under way. The man who assumed he would fold tomorrow is back.
