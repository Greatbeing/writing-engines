# Topic Assessment: The Man Who Ran a Convenience Store for Ten Years (Material: one WeChat Moments screenshot)

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> Use case ③: judge with the "Four Questions of Topic Selection" whether a topic is strong enough to write as a feature. Conclusion first: **do not write the feature now; go and fill in the interviews and the scene, and if you cannot, switch engines.**

## The Four Questions

| Question | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Ill Fit | Undecided | The screenshot shows nothing about this man that "shouldn't be". Running a convenience store for ten years is itself the normal state of things; an interview is needed to find the contrast between his record and his way of living — what he did before, say, and why he stayed |
| Change | Possible | Ten years is a natural span of time, and Moments may show the nodes: opening, expanding, the pandemic, closing. But nodes are not change; the change has to be spoken by him, in his own words |
| Scene | **No (fatal)** | There is no interview and no observation in the shop. A feature's opening frame, its quotations and its details all depend on being there; write from a screenshot alone and every detail fails the question "how do you know?" |
| Pressure of the Era | Possible | Community retail being squeezed by food delivery and instant delivery is a real problem, but it needs his own situation to carry it; a public issue cannot be made to speak on his behalf |

Of the four, only "Change" and "Pressure of the Era" just barely hold; "Scene" does not hold at all. By the workflow, at least three questions must answer "yes / there is" before you write. This topic **does not pass the threshold**.

## Why "no scene" is the fatal item

1. **Quotations cannot be verified.** Moments text is written for people he knows, not as an answer to an interview; lifting it straight into a piece as a quotation means changing, on his behalf, the occasion on which he was speaking.
2. **Details cannot be traced.** The shelves, the cat, the lamp at night in that screenshot: you do not know which year it was, which shop it was, or whether he took the picture.
3. **Ethical risk.** Using a person's private content as a public feature without his consent invades privacy, and it breaks the red line that forbids polishing a subject's words.

## Alternatives (choose one)

1. **Add the interviews first, then come back and assess.** At minimum: more than two face-to-face interviews; one full business day of on-site observation, from opening to closing; two or three corroborating voices (a regular customer, a supplier, a family member). Once that is in place, run the four questions again — the "Ill Fit" question can usually only be found inside the interview.
2. **Switch engines.** If only second-hand material is available:
   - Rewrite it as a **living-person city observation** (sanlian-writing): write not about him alone but about the type, "the owners of neighbourhood convenience stores", using several real samples, and with no named close portrait of one man;
   - Or make it a **commentary** (guanfang-writing): enter from the survival situation of small neighbourhood shops and discuss what instant retail is doing to corner business, using the person only as a hook for the scene, anonymised and with his consent.

## One Line

A screenshot and no scene: this is not yet a feature, it is a lead. Go and sit in the shop for an evening first.
