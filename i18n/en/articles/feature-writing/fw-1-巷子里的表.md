# Lao Fan Takes Time Apart

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> Trial draft. The character "Fan Guoliang" is invented; the alley name, the dates and the quotations are invented for demonstration and must be replaced with material from real interviews before publication [to be verified].

In a city where everyone looks at a phone, Fan Guoliang has made a living repairing watches for ten years. That fact in itself is a little out of order.

On a morning in November 2026, half the window of the tin kiosk at the mouth of Xiliu Alley was open. Fan Guoliang wore a single-lens loupe, and his tweezers held a screw smaller than a sesame seed. Someone knocked on the window and passed in an old Shanghai-brand watch. He did not look up. "Leave it there."

## A Folding Table

Fan Guoliang was born in 1971. At sixteen he entered the county's watch and clock factory, and what he learned was assembly. The factory closed in 2003; he went south and worked as a security guard, delivered bottled water, drove a hoist on building sites. In the spring of 2016 he came back to this city and set up a folding table at the alley mouth below his sister's building.

On the table: a piece of felt, a desk lamp, a box of screwdrivers. In his first month he repaired nine watches.

"Back then I thought, I'll do this until I can't do it any more," he says. "I never thought I'd still be at it now."

## Slow

To service a mechanical watch he takes out more than a hundred parts and lays them on the felt in a circle. Cleaning the oil, re-oiling, regulating: two days at the fastest.

Some customers complain that it is slow. He pushes the watch back across the table: "In a hurry? Buy a new one."

In 2019 the alley was scheduled for redevelopment and the subdistrict replaced the kiosks as a set. He chose the position furthest in, because the afternoon sun does not reach it. "Sun on it and the oil thins."

His fingers are thick and his nails are cut very short. There is an old scar on the index finger of his left hand, left by a press in the factory. He says the scar does not get in the way: "The tweezers are in my right hand."

## The People Who Come

Most people who come to have a watch repaired are not there to tell the time.

A girl brought in her grandfather's watch. It had stopped at ten past three. She would not let him reset the time; she only wanted it running again. Fan Guoliang worked on it for five days and took no money. "It's an old watch. I know this movement."

A middle-aged man comes once a year to change a battery, eight yuan. He stands and waits every time, says "Thank you, master" when it is done, and says nothing else. Fan Guoliang does not ask who he is.

In 2022 the alley was sealed for three months. He came anyway, every day: opened half the window, sat for a while, locked up again.

"If I don't come, my hands forget the work."

## The Price List

On the kiosk's windowsill there is a price list written in ballpoint on a piece of cardboard: battery change, eight yuan; strap change, from fifteen; cleaning and servicing of a mechanical watch, one hundred and twenty. The card was written in 2016 and has been changed once in ten years — in 2021, when servicing went from one hundred to one hundred and twenty.

"Parts went up, oil went up," he says. "Labour didn't."

He buys parts at a wholesale market two hundred kilometres away, twice a year, taking the six o'clock bus in the morning and coming back with a backpack. Some of the gears for the old movements went out of production long ago, so he strips them out of junk watches he has bought, sorts them by model into medicine bottles, and writes on each bottle with a marker on tape. On the wooden shelf at the very back of the kiosk there are more than forty such bottles.

In the summer of 2023 a boy from a vocational school asked whether he could learn from him. The boy came every Saturday, sat on a low stool beside him, learned to take off a battery cover first and then to know the screws. After four months the boy went to work at a phone repair shop. Fan Guoliang says that is fine too: "He earns more over there." When the boy left, he gave him a pair of tweezers he had used for twenty years — the tips worn bright, a strip of black tape wound around the handle.

He tells this quickly, and when he has finished he puts his head down to turn a screw, and turns it for a long while.

## Ten Years

This year he is fifty-five. His eyes have gone; the loupe has gone from 3x to 5x. Three watches a day at most; any more and his hands shake.

His daughter works in another city and has urged him to go home. He says he will see.

Asked how much longer he means to go on, he thinks for a moment and says: "People still wear watches."

A little after five in the evening, the street lamps in the alley come on first. He puts the parts on the felt back into their tin boxes one by one; on each lid is a name and a date in his own hand. The last is the Shanghai watch. It is running now. He sets it on the windowsill, listens for a while, and closes the window.
