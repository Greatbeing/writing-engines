# The Knife Sharpener

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> Demonstration draft; the material is invented, for testing only. Composite composition.
> Atom chain: Thing-First Opening → One Line Makes a Person → Action for Thought (a craft) → Classical Bones → The Way in the Common → Folk Wisdom → Cold Close

Once a month a knife sharpener comes to our yard, carrying a long bench, with two whetstones nailed to one end of it.

He does not call out when he comes in. He sets the bench down under the locust tree, straddles it, takes a piece of iron out of his coat, and strikes the bench leg three times. Tang. Tang. Tang. Doors open, and people come out with their knives.

His surname is Sun. His left hand is missing two fingers. Someone asked about it once. He said: "From sharpening." The one who had asked did not ask again.

Sharpening begins with looking. He turns the knife sideways, squints one eye, and sights along the edge. That done, he scrapes the pad of his thumb along the edge and says one word: "Blunt." Or: "Rolled." A rolled edge has to be hammered first. He draws a small anvil out from under the bench, lays the knife on it, and taps with a small hammer, tap by tap, very lightly, as if he were hammering someone asleep.

Then the coarse stone. The stone is watered first, water poured from an army canteen. The blade lies against the stone face, tilted a little, pushed out, drawn back. Force going out, no force coming back. Thirty strokes on one side, turn it over, thirty. He does not count, but it is thirty every time. I counted.

The coarse stone done, the fine stone. The fine stone is blue-grey and slick, and the sound it makes is finer too, shhh, shhh. At the very end he lifts the knife and holds it up against the sun: one white line along the edge. He pulls a hair from his head, lays it on the edge, and blows, and the hair parts in two.

"There."

A finished knife he does not hand over directly. Spine outward, handle toward the person, held level across both hands, and he lets go only when the other has it steady. My mother took it and said: "Thank you for the trouble, Master Sun." He nodded, said nothing, and bent his head to the next knife.

He does not go home at noon. Out of his bundle come two steamed buns and one scallion, and he squats at the edge of the bench to eat. The bun is split, a length of scallion pinched into it, one bite, chewed a long time. When he is done he picks the crumbs off his knees and puts them in his mouth.

Five yuan a knife. Cleaver, five. Scissors, three. Axe, eight. The price does not change. If someone says it is steep, he does not argue; he hands the knife back and goes on to the next one.

Old Ma's family had a knife his father had left behind. The steel was hard, and nobody could sharpen it properly. Master Sun worked at it for half an hour, and when it was done he would not take money. Old Ma insisted. He waved his hand.

"This knife is good. Sharpening it, I am the one who comes out ahead."

Afterwards Old Ma told people: this one has some 道行 in him. [Note: 道行, dàoheng — literally the cultivation a practitioner has built up over years; here, roughly, "the man has the real thing in him." No English word carries the religious-craft double sense, so the term is left standing.]

That year I was ten. I squatted by the bench and watched, the whole afternoon. He finished the last knife, put the stones away, looked at me, and said: "A knife is not afraid of going blunt. It is afraid that nobody uses it."

I did not understand it. I nodded.

He shouldered the bench and went off. At the yard gate he struck the iron three more times. Tang. Tang. Tang.

Later I went to middle school and did not see him much. The house changed to stainless steel knives. They said they did not need sharpening.
