# A Bowl of Rice

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> Rewriting demonstration: keyed to the source lines of test-prompt ac-3, in which every psychological state and every set phrase has been replaced by an action.
> Atom chain: Thing-First Opening → Action for Thought → How People Eat → Plain Dialogue → Cold Close. The material is invented; composite composition.

He had not eaten one whole meal in three days.

Before the food came he sat on the bench, his back very straight, both hands pressed down on his knees.

The food came: a bowl of coarse rice, with a few slices of pickled vegetable laid over the top. He did not move. He looked first. He looked for a while, then pulled the bowl toward himself, pulled it as far as the edge of the table, and put both hands around it.

The hand that took up the chopsticks was shaking. He tapped the chopsticks level against the table, tapped twice, and only then reached them into the bowl.

The first mouthful was a big one. In it went, his cheeks bulging, no chewing, stopped. He stopped for a moment, and then chewed slowly, chewed very fine. When he swallowed, his throat moved once, and his eyes closed once.

After that it went fast. Mouthful after mouthful, the chopsticks scraping around the inside of the bowl, pickled vegetable and rice going in together. Down at the bottom, he stood the bowl up on end and scraped the grains stuck to its wall down one by one, brought them to the edge, and licked them off.

At the end he turned the bowl over, looked at the bottom of it, turned it back the right way, and set it down.

Someone asked whether he wanted more.

"Enough."

His hand was still resting on the bowl.

[Note: 糙米 is coarse, unmilled rice, and 咸菜 is plain salted vegetable — the cheapest meal there is. The Chinese gives the two nouns and no adjective at all; English has to name them, so they are named plainly and left to do the same work.]

[Note: This piece is a demonstration of one method. In the original, every inner state and every ready-made phrase has been swapped out for an action: hunger is a shaking hand, relief is one closing of the eyes, thanks is a hand that stays on the bowl after the answer. Nothing is named, and nothing is explained. English can follow the actions, but its habit is to reach for the feeling just after them; the translation refuses that reach and stops where the Chinese stops.]
