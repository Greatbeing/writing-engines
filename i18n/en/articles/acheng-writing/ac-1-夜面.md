# Night Noodles

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> Demonstration draft; the material is invented, for testing only. Composite composition.
> Atom chain: Thing-First Opening → One Line Makes a Person → Plain Dialogue → How People Eat → Folk Wisdom → Action for Thought → Heavy Things Lightly → Cold Close

The factory's night shift lets out at twelve. Out the gate, two hundred steps east, there is a noodle stand under the bridge arch.

The stand is a three-wheeler. In the bed are stacked the stove, the pot, a pile of bowls. Nobody has ever asked the owner's surname; everyone calls him Old Geng. Old Geng does not talk. When someone comes he lifts his chin at the stool — that counts as a greeting.

The first time I went was after two shifts back to back. Standing by the cart, my legs were soft. Old Geng looked at me once.

"Big bowl?"

"Big bowl."

"Egg?"

I felt in my pocket and said no egg. He did not ask again. He took a handful of noodles and put them in the pot.

The noodles are alkaline noodles, yellow, stiff. The water was boiling; the noodles went in; he stirred them once with long chopsticks, put the lid on, and went to cut scallions. The scallions were cut fine, the knife sounding its way across the board. When that was done he lifted the lid; the noodles had already floated up. He scooped them with a strainer, shook it three times, tipped them into the bowl. A ladle of bone broth, the scallions scattered, two drops of oil. The bowl was pushed over, and pressed underneath it was a poached egg.

I looked at him. He was wiping the pot.

I carried the bowl to the stool and did not touch the chopsticks at first. Both hands went around the rim of the bowl. The bowl was hot, hot enough to numb the palms, and I did not let go. Only when my palms were hot through did I pick up the chopsticks.

Soup first. The soup was salty, and under the salt was the rankness of bone. One mouthful, then a pause, letting it go down the throat, down into the belly; and then there was a lump of heat in the belly. Then the noodles. They could not be sucked in at one go. They had to be lifted one chopstickful at a time, raised, rested against the rim so the broth dripped back, and then carried to the mouth. Chew. Chew until the noodle had no spring left in it, then swallow.

The egg was left for last. The yolk was runny. I broke it and let the yolk run into the broth that was left, lifted the bowl, and drank broth and yolk down clean. There were a few grains of scallion at the bottom; I nudged them into my mouth one at a time with the chopsticks.

Finished, I sat. A truck went over the bridge, and the arch hummed.

On the next stool sat an old man, also from the factory, the gatekeeper. He ate more slowly than I did. When he was done he wiped his mouth with his sleeve and said: "A man, at night, eats one bowl of something hot — and that day hasn't been wasted."

I said yes.

Old Geng took the bowl and rinsed it in an iron bucket. I got out my money, got out eight yuan. He took it and gave back two.

"The egg?"

"There was one extra in the pot."

I held the two yuan in my hand and held them a while, then put them in my pocket.

After that I went often, on night shift. Old Geng still did not talk. Sometimes there was an extra egg, sometimes not.

Old Geng's stove burned honeycomb coal. A lump of coal has twelve holes in it; burned through, it is red. He would take it out with the tongs, throw it into a tin bucket under the cart bed, put in a new one, and set the holes true, hole to hole. When he changed the coal he lifted the pot aside — the broth must not go cold — and covered the lid with a wet rag. When it was done the pot went back, the rag was wrung out, and hung on the handlebar.

He packed up at two. First he stacked the stools one by one and tied them to the side of the cart with rope; then he stacked the bowls; last he turned the stove door down small, leaving one crack. The three-wheeler pedalled away, and under the bridge arch it was dark.

That winter the factory stopped. I went to the arch once. The three-wheeler was not there. On the ground there was a black patch, where the stove had burned.

I stood there a while, and walked back.
