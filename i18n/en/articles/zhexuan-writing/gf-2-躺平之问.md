# The "Lying Flat" Question: the Answer Is Not in Bed

"I can't 卷 [Note: juǎn, "to roll" — from 内卷, nèijuǎn, "involution": grinding harder in a competition that no longer pays] any more; I want to 躺平." The line turns up often on young people's social platforms. Some call it self-mockery, some a manifesto, and some worry: have the young stopped wanting to strive?

One fair word first: most of those who say they want to lie flat are not lazy but tired. The programmer working past midnight, the graduate sitting the postgraduate exam three years running, the older single going to five blind dates a month — who has not, at some moment, thought: forget it. This exhaustion deserves to be seen.

So have the young really all lain down? The numbers show another side. A 2025 questionnaire by a university research team [to be verified] found that among respondents who call themselves "wanting to lie flat", over seventy per cent still put in forty-plus hours a week of work or study; nearly half are learning new skills in their spare time. The mouth says lie down; the body is still running.

The question therefore has to be rephrased. Rather than ask whether young people should lie flat, ask: what is it that made 躺平 [Note: tǎngpíng, "lying flat" — the online term for opting out of the rat race] a high-frequency catchphrase?

Take it apart. Some employers package "996" [Note: jiǔ jiǔ liù — nine in the morning to nine at night, six days a week] as "福报" [Note: fúbào, "blessing" — the boss's word for overwork being a reward]; some job ads draw a hard line at thirty-five; some platforms turn workplace anxiety into a traffic business. These concrete practices really are overdrawing young people's patience. They deserve criticism, and they deserve fixing.

At the same time, it should be seen that encouraging work, protecting rights, and letting those who strive be rewarded remains the direction our society holds to. Blaming everything on "the times" does not fit the facts; pushing all the responsibility onto the young is not fair either.

In the end, lying flat is not a choice the young have made; it is the breath they take at certain moments. One breath, so as to run a longer road.

Want to lie a while — that is allowed; want to stand up — the road is still there; want to take another road — that is no disgrace.

How to live this one life, the answer is in each person's own hands. What we can do is make that road a little smoother, and see to it that every fresh start has someone there to catch it.
