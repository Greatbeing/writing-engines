# Twenty Minutes in the Park Is Not Escape; It Is a Gap Left for Yourself

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> Trial draft. The netizen comments and figures in this piece are fictional demonstrations and must be replaced with verifiable sources before publication [to be verified].

On the way home from work, turn into the neighbourhood park, find a bench, do nothing, sit for twenty minutes. Lately the "公园20分钟效应" [Note: gōngyuán èrshí fēnzhōng xiàoyìng, "the park twenty-minute effect" — the online name for the lift people report from a short, aimless sit in a park] has gone viral; plenty of young people post their "park check-ins": one ginkgo leaf, one dog going past, one cup of hot soy milk carried over from the convenience store.

One netizen wrote: "After a day at work my head is like a drawer packed full; sit in the park a while and the drawer finally shuts." That sentence says what a great many people feel. Tired — that is real; wanting a breath — that is real too.

Why the park, of all places? It is worth taking apart.

First, it is near. Many cities are pushing "口袋公园" [Note: kǒudài gōngyuán, "pocket parks" — small green plots dropped into built-up blocks], so a patch of green is ten minutes' walk away, no ticket, no queue. Second, it is light. No gear, no plan; a pair of legs that still walk is enough. Third, it is loose. In a park there is no performance target, no message alert, no one asking whether the proposal has been revised yet.

Think about it: out of twenty-four hours, how much unbroken time is left for yourself? The commute takes a stretch, work takes a bigger one, and scrolling the phone quietly takes another. The twenty minutes in the park are gouged out of those gaps.

Some say this is young people running away. Better, really, to ask not why they run to the park but whether our cities have left people enough places where they can stop.

What deserves the questioning is the concrete practice — fencing green space off and charging for it, redesigning benches so that no one can lie on them, leaving a park with nothing but a "look at it" function. That urban planning should put people at the centre is a principle long since written into documents; it only lands when it lands on one bench, one patch of lawn.

The park craze — what is hot is not the park but people re-weighing the rhythm of their own lives. It is not 躺平 [Note: tǎngpíng, "lying flat" — the online term for dropping out of the rat race]; it is pressing pause inside a fast tempo.

So there is no need to put a label on the trend. If you want to go, go and sit; if you have no time, you can also stand a while by a window.

One more bench in the city, and life has one more place to settle; one more blank stretch in the day, and the heart has a little more room.
