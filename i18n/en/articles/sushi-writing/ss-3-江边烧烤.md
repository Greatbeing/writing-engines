# Barbecue by the River

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Nine on a Saturday night, Old Zhou asked me out for barbecue by the river. The stall is set up below the embankment; the plastic stools are so low your knees come up to your chin, and which way the smoke drifts depends entirely on the river wind's mood.

Two bottles of beer down, Old Zhou stopped talking. He stared at the water for a long while and said: "I am thirty-eight this year. Twenty years left on the mortgage. The company has just cut a batch — I was not among them, but I did not get a raise either. You look at this river: east it runs, all year round [Note: 一年到头, "from the year's one end to the other." A flat four-character phrase, and exactly how a man states a fact he is tired of.], and it has been at it a few thousand years without tiring. I have put in a dozen, and this is what I have muddled to."

He had used the verb for muddling along. [Note: 混, to muddle along, to drift — set against the river's 流, "to flow." The two sit one sound apart; the complaint rests on that gap, and English, needing two unrelated words, loses it.]

There was sense in that, and I could not argue with it.

I thought a while and said: "Look at this water. It is always moving, never stops for a moment — and it is always here too, it has not gone anywhere. Counted by a single year, you really have not moved forward much. Counted by these dozen years, you have carried a whole family on your back. Is that not like the river as well — seeming not to move, while it runs the whole time?"

Old Zhou said nothing. He picked up a skewer of grilled chives and chewed for a long while. A cargo boat went slowly down the river; the horn sounded once, and the ripples pushed in layers to the bank and drew back. Watching the boat, he suddenly smiled and said that was about what his father had told him back then.

Across the water the buildings had their lights coming on floor by floor; they say one flat there costs what the two of us would earn in a lifetime. Those are not ours; thinking about them is a waste. But this river wind is for anyone to breathe, and the smoke off the skewers is for anyone to be smoked by, and the owner gave us two extra slices of grilled mantou.

We drank a few more bottles after that and the talk wandered further and further off, from our primary-school form teacher to who we had had crushes on in middle school. By the time the owner was stacking the stools it was nearly one. Old Zhou stood up, his leg had gone numb, he staggered and caught hold of me, and said: "Next time we come again." I said next time you pay — you ate three more skewers of kidney than I did.
