# Notes on a Transfer

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

On a Monday early in March, HR called me in and said they were moving me to the archive room. It was put very politely; the gist was that I had rich experience and was suited to being "settled" [Note: 沉淀, literally "to let the sediment settle." The corporate euphemism for being parked somewhere quiet with nothing to do.]. I understood. I nodded and said fine.

The archive room is on the basement level, next to the transformer room, which hums the year round [Note: 一年四季, "the four seasons"; the four-character phrase makes the humming sound permanent, which is the point.]. No window, only rows of steel cabinets, and when a door opens a smell of old paper comes out. My old desk had been by a window, with the ginkgo downstairs in view. Now it was a fluorescent tube, one of them flickering on and off.

The first week was the worst. Before, three meetings a day and eighty messages to answer, the phone never still; now a whole morning passed with nobody looking for me. I scrolled the work group three times over and found everyone discussing a project I was no longer part of. At noon in the canteen I ran into old colleagues, who asked politely, "Quiet over there, is it?" I said quiet, very quiet indeed [Note: 清闲, "clear and idle," the standard word for a post with light duties; said about a person, it can mean sidelined.]. On the way back I stood in the lift a long time before pressing the floor. At night, lying in bed, I went through the projects of the past few years one by one in my head, and the more I went through them the less I slept; finally I got up and dug out the photo of the pothos on my old desk. It was still upstairs. It had not come down with me.

By the second week I had begun numbering the steel cabinets. Somewhere in the numbering I turned up a bundle of reimbursement slips from the nineties, handwritten, each hand finer than the last. One read: "Entertaining client — one dish of peanuts, two bottles of beer," with three signatures after it. I laughed at that slip for a good while, thinking that those three, back then, had probably also been worrying over a single dish of peanuts.

After that I began rather to like the room. Tidying in the morning; in the afternoon, a pot of tea and the old slips. Heard long enough, the hum from the transformer room sounds like someone next door reciting sutras. Off at half past five on the dot; out of the building, and the sky is still light. In all my years here it was the first time I had seen the sunset colours on the way home before dark.

The projects upstairs, the meetings — lively enough, but none of it mine any more. I do not think about it. Yet this sunset costs nothing; the ten minutes of wind between the office and the subway station cost nothing; nobody can transfer those away from me. I look every day. Sometimes red, sometimes purple. A month and more of it, and I have seen more than in the whole year before.

Come to think of it, the people upstairs are run off their feet [Note: 脚不沾地, "feet do not touch the ground," the set phrase for that] — where would they find time to look at this? Someone "settled" like me, off work on the dot with the leisure to watch the sky: you could search the whole building and not find a second.

A couple of days ago an old colleague came down to the basement for an old contract and sneezed the moment he came in. I found it for him and handed him a cup of tea. He took a sip and said, "It is rather comfortable down here." I said it is comfortable, except the tube keeps flickering, so people look like something out of a ghost film.
