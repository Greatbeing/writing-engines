# Working Late, Into Snow

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

December fourth, a Friday. The last version of the proposal was finished at twenty past one in the morning. The time clock went *beep*, once, as though sighing on my behalf.

Down on the ground floor the old security guard was asleep inside his padded coat. I pushed the glass door open and stopped: everything outside had gone white. Under the streetlamp the snow came down in clumps, slowly, as if it too were in no hurry. [Note: 一团一团 — "clump by clump." The Chinese reduplication gives the fall a slow, countable rhythm that English cannot repeat without fuss.]

The ride-hailing app said forty-seven people ahead of me. The subway had long since stopped. I stood on the steps and did the arithmetic: forty minutes to walk it, and my shoes were a thin pair of single-layer pumps, and there was a hole in one sock.

On an ordinary day I could have cursed the boss, the client and the weather, one after another. That night, for some reason, watching the snow settle on the shared bikes — every one of them wearing a white hat — I suddenly laughed.

I thought: this snow is not falling specially for people who have just finished overtime. It is simply falling. Those daytime people, sitting in a heated meeting room — who among them would see it? To see this snow you had to be someone still at work at one in the morning, unable to get a cab, walking home.

Seen that way, having the proposal sent back three times did not seem like wasted work. It was the ticket money for this snow.

Counted by the day, it was a bad day. Counted by a lifetime, who remembers how many drafts of the slides were redone on December fourth? But half past one in the morning, one person walking alone through new snow on an empty street, the ground going *gezhi, gezhi* underfoot [Note: 咯吱咯吱, the creak of fresh snow packed down by a shoe; the four-syllable repetition is onomatopoeia that English "crunch" flattens to one beat.] — that, I expect, I will remember a long while.

I passed the corner where the pancake stall had shut hours before; snow lay on its tin cart too, spread more evenly than the pancakes the owner ever managed. Farther on was a pedestrian bridge with nobody on it. I stood in the middle and looked down: the whole road held only a single ribbon of tyre tracks, and the traffic lights were still changing colour in earnest. Changing for whom? For me, probably. I waved at the empty road, by way of thanks, and I do not know whether it saw me.

And so I walked home. At the gate of the compound the hole in my sock had frozen through and my toes had lost all feeling. Before going up I pressed one footprint into the snow and looked at it: rather ugly, not unlike my proposal.
