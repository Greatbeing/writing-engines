# Chapter One: A Lone Lamp on the Snow Picks Out a Stranger; a Broken Pole Across the River Knows an Old Friend

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> 第一回　孤灯照雪辨来客　断篙横江识故人 [Chapter One: a lone lamp on the snow tells a stranger; a broken pole across the river knows an old friend.]

Third month, seventeenth year of Chongzhen [1644, the year the Ming fell]. The Yellow River ice had just broken, and the floes still shoved at one another mid-channel, grinding like a millstone far off.

One lamp still burned at the Mengjin ferry, hung from the awning of Han Laoqi's boat. Han Laoqi sat beneath it mending his net. Past sixty, half his left ear gone, he was steady: the shuttle never caught a second strand. Everyone at the ferry knew his boat did not go out after dark, for any money.

That night someone insisted.

The caller was a scholar of twenty-odd, his cotton gown soaked to the thigh, an ebony box lashed to his back with hemp rope. One ingot of silver went down on the bow. "Elder, put me across."

Han Laoqi looked at the silver, then at his shoes: grey brick dust, not the ferry's yellow mud. "From the capital?"

The scholar flinched and nodded.

"Folk from the capital run south. Few run north." He slid the shuttle into the net. "You go north to throw your life away, or to carry something there?"

No answer. He pulled the box tighter to his chest.

Han Laoqi sighed and cast off. The rope had barely touched the water when hoofbeats broke out of the snow on the bank. Three horses: one ahead, two behind. The leader wore grey fox fur, a long saber at his waist, never drawn. Of the two behind, one was squat as a wine jar, the other had only a right arm, his left sleeve folded into his belt.

The man in grey reined in at the edge of the lamplight. "Brother Han Seven. Twenty years, and you've learned to ferry."

The pole in Han Laoqi's hand stopped in midair.

"Shen San," he said. "Your saber still won't come out?"

"With Brother Seven, it must, once." Shen San swung down. "The man stays, the box stays, and the boat sails as usual. The ferry's silver is mine from today."

The scholar had gone white. "Elder, in the box there is—"

"Don't say it," Han Laoqi said. "Say it, and I have no choice but to take a hand."

The squat man struck first. Clumsy to look at, quick on his feet: three steps to the hull, both palms crossed and driven at it to tip boat and men into the ice river. Han Laoqi did not give ground. He touched the bank with the pole, and the boat slid three feet sideways on the borrowed force. The palms met air; he could not check himself, and one foot went into the shallows.

The second stroke came at once. The tip of the pole swept flat across the water and threw a sheet of icy spray into his face. His eyes shut; the butt came round and tapped him behind the knee. He went down on both knees in the water and did not rise.

"Three Strokes Against the Current [逆水三篙]," said the one-armed man. "Where is the third?"

"Kept for your Brother Shen."

The one-armed man said nothing. An iron chain came out of his sleeve, a fist-sized meteor hammer at its end. Twice around his head it hummed, then straight at the lamp. Put it out and only snow-light would be left. Han Laoqi was old; his eyes might not match a young man's.

But he had known. His left hand took the lantern down and pushed it into the scholar's arms: "Hold it steady." His right brought the pole up on the slant, not at the hammer but at the middle of the chain. The chain wrapped the bamboo three times around. The one-armed man hauled; the pole snapped, and half of it flew up with the chain and fell among the floes.

The scholar said, "Ah—"

Three feet of broken pole were left in Han Laoqi's hand.

Only then did Shen San draw. The blade left the sheath without a sound. Snow fell on the steel and did not melt.

"Brother Seven. At Xuanfu [the frontier garrison] thirteen of us held one beacon tower, and two came out alive. You lost half an ear; I lost a brother." He glanced at the one-armed man. "His elder brother. You said the court owed us a debt. The court is gone. I'll collect it myself."

Han Laoqi was silent a long while. Mid-channel, one floe struck another. Dong.

"Do you know what's in the box?"

"The Xuanfu rolls of the war dead, and the ledgers of pay arrears," Shen San said. "Carried to Nanjing it is a merit. Carried to the Rebel Camp [Li Zicheng's] a greater one."

"Those thirteen names are in it. Your brother's too." He turned to the one-armed man. "Your brother was Sun Dayou. He died on the ninth of the twelfth month, four days hungry, half a cake of horse-feed beans in his fist. It is all written in the roll. Sell it for reward money, and he dies a second time for nothing."

The chain hung down.

Shen San's blade did not. "Brother Seven, you plead the heart. I plead the case. In a broken world a name is worth little."

"Then we won't plead."

He kicked the scholar into the cabin and stepped ashore. The broken pole turned half a circle and came level across his chest. The opening of the third stroke. [Named, withheld, never shown.]

Ten feet apart, neither moved. Snow gathered on his ruined ear. The one-armed man had withdrawn under the willow; the squat man still knelt in the water, teeth knocking. Nobody stepped forward.

Afterwards the ferry told that night differently. Some heard one ringing clash of steel; some nothing at all. Only the dawn boatman saw: a long saber standing in the snow, broken in two; a grey fox fur in the willow; no men, no horses. Han Laoqi's boat was moored on the north bank, one ingot of silver on the bow, untouched.

The scholar became a minor official in Nanjing. Asked about that night, he said only: the old man spoke not a word on the far bank, asked for the lantern back, and hung it up again.

As for what the third stroke looked like, he said, he had been in the cabin, and had not seen it.
