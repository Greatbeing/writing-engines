# Chapter One: Three Factions at One Table Toast the New Wine; One Cup Undrained and the Old Alliance Comes Apart

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

> 第一回　三帮同席贺新酒　一杯未尽散旧盟 [Chapter One: three factions at one table toast the new wine; the cup not emptied dissolves the old alliance.]

Autumn of the twentieth year of Qianlong [1755]. The Grand Canal was thick with grain barges, and the Shen family of Qingjiangpu, in Huai'an Prefecture, had set forty tables for a wedding feast.

The Shen family was marrying a daughter to the young master of the local rice house. In the jianghu they were the Iron Steelyard Sect [铁秤门]: three generations keeping the scales for the Grain Transport Guild [漕帮], and on their beam nobody had ever been allowed to come up an ounce short. The master, Shen Huaiqiu, was fifty-eight, a short man who kept a bronze steelyard weight in his right hand the year round, turning it in his palm as he walked, turning it very evenly.

Of the guests, three parties stood out.

The first was Third Master Dou of the Grain Transport Guild. His voice carried; before he was through the gate he shouted: "Old Shen! If this wine's been watered, I'll carry the table out on my shoulder too!" Then he laughed at his own joke, and the whole courtyard heard it.

The second was the Blue Robe Society [青袍会], risen out of the salt smugglers. Their man was a lean, tall middle-aged fellow named Ke, in a blue cotton gown washed to the colour of old paper. He did not drink wine. He drank tea, and at every sip he glanced at the gate.

The third was the quietest. From Shilipu [Ten-Li Bridge] in northern Huai came an old woman on a jujube-wood staff, two young men behind her. She sat and said nothing at all, but the staff lay across her knees the whole time and never once leaned against the table.

Three rounds in, Shen Huaiqiu stood up.

He set the bronze weight in the middle of the head table. "Everyone. Today I marry off my daughter, and I will use the day to say one thing. From today the Iron Steelyard Sect keeps no more scales for the Guild. I am leaving the jianghu."

The courtyard went quiet.

Third Master Dou was first to hit the table. "Old Shen, if you quit, who keeps the scales? These years the rice price on the canal has been held down by that beam of yours!"

Mr Ke set down his tea bowl. "If Master Shen means to retire, he owes us a reason. A steelyard is dead; men's hearts are alive. If the scales are not in your hand, whose hand are they in?"

The old woman still said nothing. One of the young men behind her started to rise; she touched the back of his foot with her staff, and he sat down again.

"There is one reason," Shen Huaiqiu said. "Last winter, thirty-seven people starved to death at Shilipu. Their rice came up the canal. It passed over my beam."

Third Master Dou's face changed. "The harvest was bad!"

"The harvest was bad. That is true," Shen Huaiqiu said. "The scales were false. That is true too."

He turned the weight over. On the underside was a freshly ground gouge, bright enough to sting the eye. "This weight has been filed two mace light [about 7.5 grams]. I kept scales thirty years and never noticed."

Mr Ke's hand stopped on the tea bowl.

The courtyard turned to look at Third Master Dou. He went red and stood up to speak; Shen Huaiqiu raised a hand.

"It was not Third Dou," he said. "The weight is mine. It turned in my hand every day. A year of turning, and it wore light."

Nobody made a sound.

"My hand moved every day. I thought I was steadying the scales," Shen Huaiqiu said. "I was wearing them down. Thirty years of keeping the standard, broken by my own hand. What business has a man like that with scales?"

Only then did the old woman speak, hoarse: "Master Shen, the people of Shilipu did not come to ask you to retire. We came to ask: that two mace, who makes it good?"

Shen Huaiqiu bowed to her, deeply. "This year's dividend from the Shen rice house goes to Shilipu, all of it. My daughter's dowry has already been turned into rice. It went onto the boats last night."

The bride sat behind the curtain in the inner court and never came out. Later the kitchen help said that on that day the bride wore a single wooden hairpin. [It is the only description of her in the piece; the restraint is the point of the scene.]

Third Master Dou sat stunned a good while, then drained his bowl. "Old Shen, with you retiring like this, I don't know whether this cup counts as a wedding cup or a parting cup."

Mr Ke rose, smoothed the hem of his blue gown, bowed to Shen Huaiqiu with his hands together, said nothing, and left first.

The old woman got up on her staff, walked to the head table, picked up the bronze weight, weighed it in her hand, and put it down.

"Light," she said. "Just as well. It won't be heavy to carry from now on."

That night the lamps in the Shen courtyard burned past midnight. Someone said they saw Shen Huaiqiu sitting alone at an empty table, his palm empty, still turning.
