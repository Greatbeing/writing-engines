# Across the Table

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Monday, 8:50 in the morning. Cheng Yan, the new sales director, walked into the seventeenth-floor meeting room. He did not take the head of the table. He went to the wall first and turned the air conditioning from twenty-six degrees down to twenty-four. "When people are hot, their brains go soft," he said. "This week we close the Chengnan Hospital tender. Nobody's brain is allowed to go soft."

In the chair nearest the door sat Old Zhou. Zhou Mu was fifty-one. The last joint of his left little finger was missing, caught in a steel gate years ago when he was running construction sites. He had been with the company twenty-two years, and the contracts he had signed would paper this room. He heard Cheng Yan out, and only tightened the lid of his thermos half a turn.

Cheng Yan and Old Zhou were an old team. Ten years ago the two of them had worked the county hospitals together in an old Santana, sample machines and two cartons of instant noodles in the trunk. Cheng Yan was new then, and Old Zhou taught him the first move: don't talk price yet. Go and ask the nurses' station whether their water dispenser is broken.

Old Zhou called it "fixing the water dispenser" [修饮水机]. [The story's version of a named technique: a private move with a lineage, named once and afterwards only alluded to.] Clients do not remember your quotation. They remember who fixed their water dispenser.

This time, Old Zhou was not on Cheng Yan's side.

Last month Old Zhou had handed in his resignation and gone to the rival firm, Hengyuan. Everyone in the trade knew that Hengyuan's bid for Chengnan Hospital this time was led by Zhou Mu.

Three days before the bid opening, Cheng Yan called the team together and spread a chart on the table. On it were the eleven departments of Chengnan Hospital, and after the name of each department head was a small circle.

"Those circles are Old Zhou's," Cheng Yan said. "He started working them three years ago."

Xiao Liu asked, "So do we still have a chance?"

Cheng Yan did not answer. He took a red pen and crossed out three names, then wrote a number in the corner of the chart: eight per cent.

Round one was the clarification meeting. Hengyuan arrived first; Old Zhou sat in the second row with a kraft-paper envelope beside him. When Cheng Yan came in, Old Zhou looked up, met his eyes, and nodded, the way he had nodded in a county hospital corridor ten years before.

The head of the hospital's equipment department asked one question: how fast is after-sales response? Hengyuan said four hours. Cheng Yan's turn: two hours, with an engineer stationed on site at the Chengnan campus.

Old Zhou bent his head and wrote a few words on the envelope.

Round two came the night before the bid opening. Cheng Yan's phone rang. It was Old Zhou.

"Little Cheng. That eight per cent of yours. Did you cut it out of profit, or out of after-sales?"

Cheng Yan held the phone. Five seconds passed before he said, "Brother Zhou, that is not a question you should be asking."

"If I don't ask it, you will cut after-sales," Old Zhou said. "You can carry a resident engineer for six months. Not three years. In month seven you pull the man out, and what the hospital curses is this trade, not you personally."

It was quiet on the other end. Cheng Yan heard him screwing the thermos lid shut.

"Does Hengyuan know you are making this call?"

"No."

Cheng Yan did not sleep that night. The quotation sheet lay on the desk. Cut eight per cent, and the tender was basically his. But Old Zhou was right: the on-site engineer would not last a year. If he quoted honestly he was handing the tender to Old Zhou; if he cut the price he was gambling the hospital on the company's next quarterly budget.

He thought of the water dispenser ten years ago. That day Old Zhou had squatted at the nurses' station for forty minutes fixing it, and stood up with his trouser legs soaked through. A nurse poured him hot water. Old Zhou said thank you, turned to Cheng Yan, and said, "This one may not come through. But this cup of water is real."

Round three was the bid opening.

Cheng Yan quoted three per cent above Hengyuan. On the resident-engineer line he wrote: on site for the first six months, four-hour response thereafter.

The result came that afternoon. The tender was still his.

As the meeting broke up, Old Zhou was waiting by the lifts. The doors opened and closed, and neither of them got in.

"You beat me by nine points on technical," Old Zhou said. "That imaging system you built last year, we can't match it."

"Then that call you made—"

"I didn't make it for you." Old Zhou handed him the kraft envelope. "I made it for that water dispenser."

In the envelope was a copy of Hengyuan's internal quotation, eleven per cent below Cheng Yan's, with three years on site in the after-sales line, and under that, in pencil, one small line: Not doable.

When Cheng Yan looked up, Old Zhou had already gone into the stairwell.

The following week Hengyuan removed Zhou Mu as project lead. Cheng Yan sent word through a friend that there was still a seat at his company.

Old Zhou answered with two words: "No thanks."

Cheng Yan sat in the meeting room a long while. Then he walked to the wall and set the air conditioning back to twenty-six. [The wuxia beat sheet with no fight in it: entrance, rounds of exchange, a reversal that moves everyone, a debt settled sideways.]
