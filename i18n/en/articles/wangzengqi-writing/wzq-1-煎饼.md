# The Jianbing Downstairs

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Outside the east gate of the compound where I live there is a jianbing stand.

说是摊，其实是一辆三轮车。

*Translation:* "A stand, so they say — in fact a three-wheeler." [Note: 说是…其实…, "said to be so — but in fact", is the one classical shrug in the piece, dropped in the way a pinch of salt is dropped in. English "they call it" cannot shrug.]

In the bed of the cart is set a round iron griddle — [鏊子, àozi: the griddle a jianbing is spread on, thin iron, heated from below] — with a liquefied-gas burner under it. The owner is surnamed Zhou, from Hebei. Everyone calls him Old Zhou.

Jianbing is called different things in different places. Tianjin calls it jianbing guozi. Shandong has a kind that is spread and then dried, hard, and eaten rolled around a scallion. That is another matter. Ours is spread on the spot and soft. Guozi means youtiao, a fried dough stick; some stalls put in a thin crisp instead — [薄脆, báocuì: a sheet of fried dough, brittle enough to crackle]. Old Zhou keeps both and asks which you want. Most people want the crisp. It is loud when you bite it.

Old Zhou making a jianbing is a craft. One ladle of batter, tipped into the middle of the griddle. A T-shaped bamboo rake. One turn of the wrist against the iron and the batter opens out — round, thin enough to let the light through. He turns it a circle and a half, no more, no less. I have counted. Crack an egg, break the yolk with the rake, spread it even. Scallion, coriander; coriander you are asked about first, some people don't eat it. Flip it. Brush the sauce: sweet bean sauce one layer, chili sauce one layer. The brush is an old toothbrush with the bristles cut short. On with the crisp, fold, fold again, cut through the middle with the spatula, into a paper bag. Under two minutes, start to finish.

He has a rule: two eggs at most. A man asks for three; he won't do it. Why not? "Three eggs and it isn't a jianbing any more, it's an egg pancake." Then he laughs at his own joke, and a gold tooth shows.

His wife is there too, taking the money. She doesn't say much. The payment QR code hangs on her chest; when someone scans it she looks down at her phone and says, "It's through." In winter she wears a red knitted hat with a pompom on top, and when she looks down the pompom bobs.

I have eaten Old Zhou's jianbing for three years. In three years the price went up once, from six yuan to seven. The day it went up he taped a sheet of paper to the handlebar. On it was written:

面涨了。

*Translation:* "The flour's gone up." Three characters, and nothing else. [Note: 面涨了 — literally "flour has risen" — is three characters; the whole rhetoric of the sign is that it says no more. No apology, no explanation.]

People read it and said nothing either.

Seven to eight in the morning is his busy time. People going to work, people taking children to school, strung out in a line. There is a primary-school boy who wants his without scallion every day. Old Zhou remembers; the boy doesn't have to say it. The boy's schoolbag is wider than the boy. He takes the jianbing, bites, burns his mouth, huffs at it, and keeps walking.

Speaking of breakfast, I remember my hometown when I was small. At the corner of the street there was a woman selling [糊辣汤, húlàtāng: a thick breakfast soup, dark brown, heavy with pepper]. One bowl of it and your forehead sweats. It came with [油馍头, yóumótóu: small fried dough sticks — youtiao, except that where we are from nobody calls them youtiao]. The woman ladling it was old, and when she ladled her wrist gave a little flick, and not one drop landed on the rim of the bowl. That was many years ago. She is probably long gone.

What people eat at breakfast is not entirely breakfast. It is an hour, a place, a familiar face. Change cities and the breakfast changes, and the familiar faces change, and it takes several years before they are familiar again.

Last winter Old Zhou stayed away from his pitch for half a month. Outside the east gate there was an empty patch of ground with a ring of black marks on it, pressed there by the cart standing so long. For that half month I bought rice balls at the convenience store. Cold. They didn't taste of much. Then he came back. He said he had gone home to see to something. He didn't say what. I didn't ask.

That day's jianbing, he put in an extra sheet of crisp.

I said that was too much. He said, "Eat it."
