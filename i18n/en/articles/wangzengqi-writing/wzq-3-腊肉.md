# My Grandmother's Cured Pork

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

My grandmother made her cured pork after the winter solstice.

说是天冷了，肉才放得住。

*Translation:* "She said the cold had come, and only then would meat keep." [Note: 才放得住 — "only then does it hold" — is the plain-spoken classical turn the whole piece is built on; the method that follows is that one sentence carried out in salt.]

The meat has to be belly — [五花肉, wǔhuāròu: "five-flower meat", the layered cut, fat and lean in alternating bands]. Some people use the hind leg; my grandmother said the belly was the fragrant one. A piece is cut into strips two fingers wide and rubbed with coarse salt. Sichuan pepper is mixed into the salt, and the pepper is toasted in the wok first, until it starts to pop. When the rubbing is done the strips are packed into a big clay vat and weighted down with a slab of bluestone. How many days it cures I am not sure — about a week, I think — and it is turned over once in the middle.

When it is cured it is strung on hemp rope and hung from a beam in the kitchen, the room with the stove in it. A wood fire burns below; the smoke goes up; the meat darkens day by day. When she fed the fire, my grandmother liked to throw in a handful of cypress twigs. She said it gave the meat a smell of cypress. It hangs there until New Year.

To eat it, first soak it in hot water and scrub the soot off the outside with a loofah — [丝瓜, sīguā: the sponge gourd, whose dried fibre serves as the household scouring pad]. Then steam it. Sliced, the fat layer is translucent. It is best stir-fried with garlic sprouts. No oil goes into the wok; the meat gives out its own.

My grandmother cut cured pork with the knife held at an angle, and the slices came out thin. She would cut one slice and not put it on the plate. She would lay it in my palm and say, "Taste it — salty enough?" She already knew whether it was salty enough.

Later, in the city, I bought cured pork from the supermarket. Vacuum-packed, twenty-odd yuan a [斤, jīn: half a kilogram], and black as well. Steamed, it had no smell of cypress about it.

This year my grandmother telephoned. She said the vat is still there, and the stone slab is still there; only the beam is no good any more, because the kitchen has been changed over to a gas stove.
