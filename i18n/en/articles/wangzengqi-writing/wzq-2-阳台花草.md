# A Few Plants on the Balcony

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Our balcony faces south. It isn't big: a little over two metres long, one metre wide. Under the laundry pole, along the wall, stands a row of flowerpots. Clay pots, plastic pots, and one old crock that used to hold pickled vegetables, with a piece knocked out of its rim.

Mint first. The mint was bought at the vegetable market, two yuan a bunch. It had been bought to dress a cold dish; a few stalks were left over, stood in a yogurt bottle, and a week later they had put out white roots, so I planted them. Mint is easy to keep. It doesn't mind the soil, it only wants water. Its leaves grow in pairs, with fine teeth along the edge; rub one and your finger smells cold for the rest of the afternoon. In summer I pick a few leaves, steep them in water with two lumps of rock sugar, and drink it cold. It grows like mad. The roots run along under the soil, one pot is not enough, and the next year I split off another.

Jasmine next. The jasmine came from the flower-and-bird market, thirty-five yuan, with a dozen buds on it at the time. Jasmine is afraid of the cold; in winter it has to be carried indoors. It opens one flower at a time, white, single-petalled, and smells strongest at dusk. Where I come from, old women used to pick the flowers and thread them onto a length of fine wire into a little string, to hang on a coat button. They called it a [茉莉花球, mòlì huāqiú: a "jasmine ball", a threaded string of jasmine worn on the button]. I saw them as a child. You hardly see them now.

Then a pot of scallions. Scallions are not flowers or plants.

可也种在阳台上。

*Translation:* "And yet they are grown on the balcony all the same." [Note: 可也 — "and yet, all the same" — is three syllables of classical concession doing the work of a whole sentence. Plain English has to spend a clause on it.]

When I buy scallions I keep the root ends with their whiskers, an inch or so, bury them, water them through, and in a few days green tubes come up. When I cook noodles I pinch off two, chop them into scallion bits, and scatter them over the soup. One pot will give three or four cuttings; by the last the blades are as thin as chives, and then I stop cutting and let it flower. A scallion flower is a white fuzzy ball. Bees come to it.

The one that takes up the most room is a pot of aloe, a gift from Aunt Sun downstairs. Aunt Sun is over seventy and lives alone. Her balcony has more aloe than she has room for, and she gives it away to anyone she meets. When she gave me mine she said, "If you scald yourself, break off a piece and rub it on. Better than ointment." I said all right. Then she said, "Don't overwater it. It's a desert thing; too much water and the root rots." And then she said it again. I still haven't scalded my hand, and I haven't broken off a single piece, and it has grown stout — thick leaves, a row of soft spines down each edge.

Aunt Sun has a habit. In the morning, taking the rubbish down, she passes under our balcony and looks up. If the aloe is still there, she goes on her way. Once I moved the aloe indoors, out of the sun, and the next day she knocked at the door to ask whether I had killed it.

My wife doesn't bother much with any of this. She bothers with the mint, because in summer she drinks mint water. She pinches it from the top, and says that makes it grow out sideways and come up thicker. She read that online, and she explains it very convincingly.

Last winter, when the cold came, I forgot to bring in the jasmine. Half of it froze to death. The other half put out new leaves in spring. This year it opened seven flowers. Fewer than last year.

Yesterday afternoon it rained. Aunt Sun shouted up from below to move the aloe in out of it, or the heavy rain would ruin it. I moved it, and pinched two scallions while I was there, and that evening cooked a pot of noodles.
