# The Refrigerator

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Among people who share a flat, the closest thing is a refrigerator, and the coldest thing is the same refrigerator.

This one was left by the landlord: two doors, silver-grey, and a faded magnet on the front printed with the name of some seaside city. None of us has been there, and none of us has peeled it off. Three people live here — me, Xiao Zhou, who does design, and a young man we almost never see, whom everyone calls Room Three.

The order inside the fridge works better than the duty roster taped to the wall. Three shelves in the chiller, one apiece; nobody ever said so, and nobody ever put anything on the wrong one. The freezer is common ground, jammed with three kinds of frozen dumplings: pork and cabbage, chive and egg, sweetcorn. Which bag belongs to whom needs no label; the flavour tells you.

Xiao Zhou's shelf is always full. The yoghurts are lined up by date like primary-school children waiting for roll call; there is a box of cut fruit with a sticky note on the lid, the date written on it. That is how she does everything — even eating has to be entered in the schedule first. Room Three's shelf is almost empty: half a bottle of chilli crisp and a few cans of beer, beaded with water like a man in a cold sweat. [Note: 老干妈, "Old Godmother" — the cheap bottled chilli crisp that is a fixture of Chinese shared kitchens.]

My shelf, I am ashamed to say, is mostly the leavings of takeaway. A bowl of last night's red-braised pork, the gravy set into a layer of white fat, the colour of old newspaper. Every time I open the fridge I see it; every time I think, tomorrow; every time I shut the door.

The busiest place in the fridge is the door rack. Soy sauce, vinegar, ketchup, and a bottle of oyster sauce that nobody can remember buying. That bottle is the only true common property in the flat, precisely because nobody will own up to it. Everyone uses it; when it gets down to the last inch, everyone walks around it. It stands there at a finger's height, like a child nobody has come to collect.

The accounts between people are all kept on small things like these. Last month the washing-up liquid was Xiao Zhou's; whose turn this month, all three of us know, and none of us says. I know she is waiting. She knows that I know. In the end Room Three went down at midnight and bought a bottle and left it by the sink; the next morning we both saw it and neither of us said thank you — only that day Xiao Zhou put a box of strawberries on his shelf. Deep red, very nearly overripe, so red it was going black at the edges.

Seen this way, we are all a little stingy. In fact none of us is stingy. A person alone in a city: the flat is rented, the furniture is the landlord's, even that magnet came from the last lot of tenants. The only place that is entirely your own is half a shelf in a refrigerator. To keep watch over that half shelf is to keep watch over a small stretch of days you need not discuss with anybody.

One night I got up for water. The kitchen light was off; I opened the fridge and the small cold-white bulb came on and lit the three shelves plainly: one full, one empty, one left over. I stood and looked for a while, and it seemed to me that I was reading three medical records. The compressor hummed and stopped, and the flat went suddenly quiet, quiet enough to hear somebody next door peeling garlic.

When the lease runs out we shall move away, each of us, and the refrigerator will stay for the next lot. It does not remember who; it only remembers which shelf was always empty.

What stands between people is usually not a wall. It is a sheet of cling film: you can see through it, and you cannot smell through it.
