# Old Things

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Moving house is a small trial. Every object has to answer: on what grounds are you still kept?

A cardboard box came out from under the bed. The tape had gone yellow and had lost its stick; it came loose at a touch, like a man who has held his breath a long time and finally sighs.

On top was a wad of cinema stubs, printed on thermal paper, the writing faded to a pale grey — you could not tell which film, or with whom. I remember that in those years I kept one for every film I saw, as though keeping the stub would keep those two hours mine. Now they are whiter one after another; what is left is only paper.

Underneath was an old flip phone, the case that out-of-date pearly pink, the corners worn through to the white plastic below. The charger had long been lost. There were probably a few hundred messages inside; a few I still remember the beginnings of, and not the ends. I weighed it in my hand: lighter than in memory.

Below that, a scarf, camel-coloured, given to me by someone one winter. I did not wear it many times, because it scratched my neck; I did not give it away, because it had been given. A thing that is neither used nor thrown out is usually not a thing one cannot part with, but the self one was when it was given.

There was also an account book, from my first year of work: rent fifteen hundred; a hundred charged onto the subway card; a bowl of malatang on a Friday night, twenty-three yuan. [Note: 麻辣烫, malatang — a cheap Sichuan-style hot pot sold by the bowl and priced by weight.] I reckoned things that finely then. Looking back, the twenty-three-yuan malatang was the most extravagant item of that year.

At the bottom of the box, pressed flat, was a plastic document wallet, blue-grey, holding several tenancy agreements, each with a different address. The signatures got faster every time; the last was nothing but a scrawled hook. I cannot recall a single landlord's name. I do remember every deposit — how much was returned, how much deducted.

I threw the stubs away, kept the phone, put the scarf in the donation bag, took it out, put it back in. The account book went onto a shelf in the new flat, and stood there with a row of books still in their wrappers.

Downstairs the removals van sounded its horn twice, politely, to hurry me.

When a person moves, what he carries away is things. What he leaves behind was somebody else's to begin with.
