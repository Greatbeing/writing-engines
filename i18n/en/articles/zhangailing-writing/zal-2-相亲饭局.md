# The Blind-Date Dinner

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

At a blind-date dinner the dishes are ordered for the other person to look at, not for yourself to eat.

The man chose the restaurant: Jiangnan cooking, sixth floor of a mall, a hundred and twenty yuan a head — not dear, not cheap, the price of a man who will not be looked down on and will not be taken for a fool. He arrived ten minutes early, took the table by the window, and turned the menu through twice, as if checking a contract.

She was seven minutes late. Seven minutes was what she had calculated at the milk-tea shop downstairs: early looks eager, more than ten minutes late looks insincere. She wore an oatmeal sweater and a small pearl brooch at the collar, her mother's. Her mother had said: the first meeting must be plain, but must not look poor.

"You order," he said, pushing the menu across.

"You order, I'm not fussy," she said, pushing it back.

The menu made one round trip between them, like a document neither side will be the first to sign. In the end he ordered: squirrel mandarin fish, crab-roe tofu, stir-fried greens, and a salted-pork soup with spring bamboo. [Note: 松鼠鳜鱼, "squirrel mandarin fish" — scored and deep-fried to fan out like a tail, then covered in sweet-sour sauce; 腌笃鲜, a Jiangnan soup of salt pork and spring bamboo.] When he ordered the fish he glanced at her; she said nothing, and he knew he had got it right. There must be one expensive dish, to show sincerity; one vegetable dish, to show he does not run through money.

She watched him order and kept her own ledger. He had not asked whether she ate chilli, so he was not much good at looking after people; but he had not ordered the dearest dish, the Longjing shrimps, so he did not care to put on a show. The two entries cancelled out; on paper the books were level.

The matchmaker had already handed over both sets of particulars. He was a supervisor at a logistics firm, had a flat, eighteen years of mortgage left. She taught English in a primary school, held a permanent post, and her parents' pensions came to a little over ten thousand a month between them. [Note: 编制, a permanent state staff post — secure, pension-bearing, and in the marriage market worth a flat.] All this they knew, so neither mentioned it. They talked about the weather, about the fish here being a little sour, about the subway raising its fares again.

The fish arrived, fried to a gold that was almost yellow, under a tomato-coloured sauce so bright it looked fake, like the plastic samples in a mall window. He put a piece of the belly on her plate, the tenderest part. She thanked him and picked the bones out one by one, laying them along the edge of the bone dish, very evenly, as if handing in homework.

He noticed how carefully she picked out the bones, and thought: this is a woman who can run a household. She noticed that after giving her the belly he ate the tail himself, and thought: probably a man who will give way — then wondered whether he did it for her to see.

Both of them were watching. Both knew that the other was watching.

Her phone buzzed on the table: the matchmaker, sending a question mark. She did not answer; she turned the phone face down. He saw, and did not ask.

The soup came last, in a big clay pot, the broth the colour of milk, the bamboo in rolled chunks, the red rim of the salt pork soaked pale. He filled a bowl for her. She said that was plenty, plenty. He added one more spoonful of bamboo.

"My mother likes making this too," he said.

He paused at his own words, as though his mother should not have come up at this point. But she laughed and said, "My mother can't cook. In our house it's my father who does the cooking."

That was the only sentence that evening which was not on the ledger.

When the bill came he was quick to scan the code: four hundred and thirty-six. She said next time it would be her treat. The words "next time" were out; they both heard them, and both pretended not to.

In the lift down there were only the two of them. The stainless doors showed two blurred figures, one tall, one short, a fist's width apart. The lift pinged, the doors opened, and the mall's music poured in — a song that had been everywhere last year.

Neither had done anything wrong. People just past thirty, with little left to lose, must weigh every item. They weigh it carefully not because they are shrewd but because they are afraid.

At the entrance he asked how she was getting home. She said the subway. He said he would see her to the station. She said there was no need. So he stood at the door and watched her walk into the crowd, and the oatmeal-coloured sweater was out of sight very quickly.

A blind-date dinner is eaten in the time of one meal, and reckoned over the accounts of half a lifetime.
