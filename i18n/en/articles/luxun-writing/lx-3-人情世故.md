# On "Knowing the Ways of the World"

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Elders often say to the young: you must understand a little 人情世故 [Note: 人情世故, "human feelings and worldly affairs" — the working knowledge of how to handle people; "worldly wisdom" is the nearest phrase, though it misses the undertone of accommodation].

The advice is sincere and should not be waved off. It seems to mean three things: greet people when you meet them, and do not let a room turn awkward; leave yourself room when you speak, and do not drive a man into a corner; when someone has done you a kindness, remember to repay it. Not one of the three is bad. Whoever says this has usually been hurt by his own ignorance of it, and is genuinely afraid the young will be hurt the same way.

Let me grant the point first: all of that is right.

But the saying, passed from mouth to mouth, changes shape as it travels.

The first layer, greeting people, has since become: which side of the table you turn to first when you raise your glass, and whose rim must be held half an inch lower than whose. The greeting is still there; the person is gone. What is left is a sequence.

The second layer, leaving room, has since become: even a plainly wrong thing may not be said aloud. Thirty people in the meeting, twenty-nine of them see that the plan will not work, and only in the corridor afterwards do they say so, quietly. The room was left; nobody was left in charge.

The third layer, remembering a kindness, has since become bookkeeping: what a gift costs, to the yuan. You gave five hundred this year; next year I return six. The feeling is the same feeling, only it has been entered in a ledger — entry by entry, clearer and clearer, and colder as it clears.

A man just out of university learns these three things: the first year he is scolded less; the second year he acquires a few more dinner invitations; the third year he has learned not to speak up in meetings. The elders observe him and are reassured: the boy has grown sensible. As for the little he had originally meant to say, nobody asks about it, and in time he stops asking himself.

Following that road to the end, of the four characters 人情世故, the 人情 grows thinner year by year and the 世故 thicker. [Note: 人情, the human feeling in it; 世故, the calculation. The two halves of one set phrase pull apart.]

In the end, what the young are told to understand is usually not how to treat people. It is how not to come off worse.

Writing this, I feel a little hollow. At last week's departmental dinner I spent a long while working out the seating, and gave the best place to a man I barely know. What was in my mind at the time was not him. It was next year's review.

I suppose I too have understood a little.

How much the young ought to understand, I cannot say. I know only that after the table broke up that night, the intern who had sat at the very edge collected the empty bottles, one by one, into a crate. Nobody had told him to.
