# On "Justice by Onlooking"

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

Lately, scrolling short videos, I keep meeting one kind of scene: a man is being pointed at inside the frame, and around him stands a ring of phones.

The one filming says nothing. He only raises the phone higher and holds it steady. Lines of text crawl across the screen: 支持曝光, "support the exposure"; 这种人就该社死, "a person like this deserves 社死" [Note: 社死, "social death" — to be shamed out of public life altogether; a recent coinage, with no English word that carries its finality]; 已转发, "already shared." The likes climb to thirty thousand in two minutes. The comment section outnumbers the people on the spot a thousand to one.

This is a remarkable kind of justice. No need to go out; no need to establish what happened; no need to take on the least involvement. The thumb moves once, and you are standing on the right side. Standing there so quickly, so neatly, that even the man passing sentence must feel outdone.

I call this kind of person a 举屏客 [Note: literally a "screen-raiser," coined on the pattern of 看客, the "onlooker" of old street and execution-ground scenes; the word keeps that ancestry visible in Chinese, which "bystander" cannot.]

The screen-raiser has a few fixed traits. First, he always comes early and leaves early. Three hours after the video goes up he has finished cursing it; on the third day another clip appears saying the thing was not as told, and by then he is raising his screen somewhere else, and does not come back to look. Second, he is never on the spot. The people on the spot have to pull the fight apart, give statements, be pressed with questions; all he has to do is share. Third, when he curses, his tone is cleaner than anyone's, as though all the dirt in the world were on the far side of the screen and on his side there were only one washed finger. Fourth, his memory is very short. Whom he cursed last month, and whether he cursed the wrong man, he does not remember; he remembers only that he was angry that day, and that the anger was entirely justified.

Leafing through old books: in the old cities, when a man was executed, the ground by the execution pit was always packed. Some stood on tiptoe; some brought their children; some, after it dispersed, would sit in a teahouse half the afternoon describing what the man's face had looked like at the end. There were no phones then, and the onlooker had only his eyes. Now he has a phone: he can keep it, set it to music, and post it again.

A hundred and more years on, the execution ground has moved into the screen. The onlooker's seat has not shifted an inch.

In the end, what justice-by-onlooking wants is not fairness. It wants a man it can safely curse.

Someone will say: exposure online has helped plenty of people; some things would never be dealt with if they were not made loud. That is true. I have seen a man owed months of wages get his money back off one video. But behind that video, someone went and checked the accounts, someone made the calls, someone knocked on the door. What the screen-raiser does is this: before the door is knocked open, he lays the man's name, his address, and his child's school out flat in the comment section.

I am not clean myself. Last month a video came up in my feed; I watched fifteen seconds and put a like under it. Later the thing turned around. I went back to find that like, and could no longer tell which one it was.

In those fifteen seconds of mine, I was holding a screen up too.

The next time a surrounded man appears on my phone, I do not know whether my thumb will be quick again. I know only this: the man standing in the middle cannot see our faces.
