# On "Received"

*Translated from the Chinese; the techniques are annotated where English cannot carry them.*

In the office group chat, one phrase has lately come into fashion: "Received."

The moment a notice goes out, a column of "Received" lines up beneath it, neat as a drill. On a long day it fills two screens, like a row of sentries at their posts.

This is a truly remarkable efficiency. A notice sent at 5:40 on a Friday afternoon has, by 5:41, been received by twenty people. As for what was received — whether it was a report to file, money to hand in, or a meeting on Monday — that is another matter, and need not be looked into too closely.

I call these people the Received School. The Received School never objects and never asks. Their thumbs are faster than their eyes; before the first line of the message is read, "Received" has already gone out. When the meeting ends, one of them turns to his neighbour: "What day did they say it's due?"

Some will say: replying "Received" is courtesy, is attitude; it is better than not replying. Quite right. But when courtesy has shrunk to a single word, and attitude exists only to be seen, then the difference between it and not replying comes down to that single word.

There are those who do not reply. The one who does not reply gets a phone call the next day, asking whether he has seen the group. He says he has. The voice says: then why didn't you reply? He wants to explain that the date in the notice was wrong, that he was about to ask. The line has already gone dead. From then on he too replies "Received," and nobody asks again whether the date is right. On the day itself, twenty people who had received it all go to the wrong place together, stand at the door of an empty meeting room, look at one another, say nothing, take out their phones, and post one more line in the group: "Received. Noted the change of date."

In the end, "Received" is not a reply. It is a declaration of position: I am here, I have not fallen behind, and if anything goes wrong, leave me out of it.

Leafing through old books, one finds that at the end of Qing-dynasty memorials to the throne the emperor often wrote three characters: 知道了, "Known." Three hundred years on, the one who writes has changed, and so have those written to; only "Known" has become "Received," and what once went from the top down now goes from the bottom up. One character fewer, and the meaning much the same. [Note: 知道了 is three characters and 收到 two, hence "one character fewer"; the joke rests on the character count, which English word counts cannot reproduce.]

No one in particular is to blame. The notice is passed down layer by layer, and by the end nobody knows what was wanted in the first place, only that not replying will not do. So everyone replies. The more who reply, the fewer dare not to; the fewer dare not to, the less those words are worth.

Writing this, I feel a little guilty. Last week's notice — I replied "Received" to it too, and to this day I have not opened the attachment.

I do not know where this will lead. I only know that the next time the group chimes, my thumb will probably move first.
